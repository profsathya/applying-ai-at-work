# Design Principles

These principles guide every design decision in this certificate. They are not aspirational — they are constraints.

## Experience before explanation

Participants encounter the gap or insight before being told what to call it. If a sprint introduces a new way of thinking about a problem, the participant should feel the need for that thinking before the framework is named. This respects the fact that working professionals have well-developed intuitions — the course builds on those intuitions rather than overwriting them with terminology.

## Real over simulated

Real problems from real workplaces, real stakeholders, real consequences. Simulation is a fallback, not a default. Working professionals have access to actual organizational problems and actual people affected by those problems. Course design should use that access, not replace it with case studies or hypothetical scenarios.

## Frameworks present, not labeled

The CTI frameworks are in the bones of the design. Working professionals develop the capabilities described by those frameworks — self-directed learning, integrative problem solving, adaptive building — but they do not need to memorize the names. The frameworks shape what participants do, not what they recite.

## AI as partner, not tool

Designing for genuine human-AI partnership, not productivity-enhanced individual work. The failure mode is treating AI as a faster way to do what you already do. The goal is a working relationship where both sides contribute things the other genuinely cannot. Course design should make that partnership visible, not assumed.

## Leverage institutional knowledge

The participant's existing knowledge of their organization is the substrate. They know their stakeholders, their processes, their constraints, and their politics. The course adds AI direction capability on top of that knowledge. It does not ask them to set aside what they know and learn AI in the abstract.

## Designed for the audience that shows up

Working professionals who chose to be there. They are evaluating whether the course is worth their time, and they have the option to stop. Design for engagement and value delivery, not for retention mechanics. Don't over-engineer for dropout scenarios in early cohorts — focus on making the experience genuinely valuable for the people in the room.
