# Self-Determination Theory in Course Design

Self-Determination Theory (SDT) identifies three psychological needs that drive intrinsic motivation: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. This certificate designs for all three deliberately.

## Autonomy

Participants choose their own problem from their own workplace. They are not all working the same scenario, following the same case study, or solving the same prompt. The problem they work on for 10 weeks is theirs — chosen because it matters to them and their organization.

This means the course must be designed to accommodate a wide range of problem types and organizational contexts. Sprint activities and assessments should be structured enough to guide without being so specific that they constrain the participant's choice. The orientation week is where the autonomy moment happens: participants survey their workplace, identify a real problem, and commit to it.

## Competence

Visible, calibrated evidence of growing capability throughout the course, not just at the end. Participants should be able to feel their own progress — not because the course tells them they are progressing, but because they can do things in Sprint 3 that they could not do in Sprint 1.

This means assessment and feedback should be woven throughout, not concentrated at the end. Each sprint should produce something the participant can point to as evidence of capability. The capstone is the culminating competence moment, but it should feel like a natural conclusion to a visible arc, not a sudden test.

## Relatedness

Real human connection — real stakeholders in their workplace, real peers in the cohort. Not simulated, not AI-roleplayed.

Relatedness is the most commonly under-designed of the three needs. It is easy to design for autonomy (let people choose) and competence (give feedback). Designing for authentic relatedness in an asynchronous online format is harder, and therefore more important to get right.

The Integrative Solver meta-habit specifically depends on authentic relatedness. A participant cannot develop the capability to synthesize across stakeholder perspectives without actually talking to stakeholders. AI can simulate a conversation, but it cannot simulate a relationship. The course must create structures that require real human interaction — with workplace stakeholders, with peers, and potentially with instructors or mentors.

Relatedness is a design priority for this certificate. It should receive explicit attention in every sprint design, not be treated as something that will happen naturally.
