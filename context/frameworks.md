# Frameworks

The CTI frameworks inform course design at three layers. Each layer builds on the one below it.

## Layer 1 — The Foundation: Read, Write, Listen, Talk

These are not communication skills. They are the mechanisms of thought itself.

Reading closely is how a person engages with existing knowledge — not skimming for keywords, but understanding structure, argument, and implication. Writing is how a person constructs and clarifies their own thinking — not documenting what they already know, but discovering what they think through the act of writing. Listening is how a person takes in perspectives that differ from their own — not waiting for their turn to speak, but genuinely processing what someone else is saying. Talking is how a person tests ideas against other minds — not presenting polished conclusions, but thinking out loud and refining through dialogue.

Without these, no amount of AI partnership produces real value. A person who cannot read closely will accept AI output uncritically. A person who cannot write to think will not know what to ask AI to do. A person who cannot listen will miss stakeholder needs. A person who cannot talk through ideas will build in isolation.

Course design should treat Read, Write, Listen, and Talk as the substrate everything else rests on, and create explicit moments where participants exercise each.

## Layer 2 — The Three Meta-Habits: SDL, IS, AB

These are not skills to acquire. They are how a capable practitioner operates in an AI-augmented environment.

**Self-Directed Learner (SDL)** identifies what they don't know, designs how to learn it, and uses AI as a learning partner — not a shortcut around learning. When a working professional encounters a problem outside their expertise, SDL is the habit that drives them to close that gap deliberately rather than delegating the understanding to AI. The difference matters: a person who delegates understanding to AI becomes dependent on it, while a person who uses AI to learn becomes more capable over time.

**Integrative Solver (IS)** synthesizes across stakeholders, domains, and perspectives. This is not abstract systems thinking — it requires authentic relatedness with real people. An integrative solver talks to the operations team and the finance team and the end users, holds all of those perspectives simultaneously, and finds solutions that account for genuine tensions. AI can help map and organize those perspectives, but the human relationships and judgment about what matters most are irreplaceable.

**Adaptive Builder (AB)** tests assumptions, builds prototypes, pivots on evidence, and iterates toward working solutions. This is not just agile methodology — it is a disposition toward action and evidence over planning and speculation. An adaptive builder ships something small, learns from what happens, and adjusts. AI accelerates the build-test-learn cycle, but the human decides what to test and what the results mean.

These three meta-habits work together. None of them stands alone, and all of them rest on Layer 1. A self-directed learner who cannot integrate perspectives builds the wrong thing. An integrative solver who cannot build anything stays in analysis. An adaptive builder who cannot direct their own learning repeats mistakes.

## Layer 3 — Supporting Frameworks

**Symbiotic Thinking** treats AI as a cognitive partner, not a tool. The human and AI each contribute things the other cannot, and the result is greater than either alone. This is the conceptual foundation for how participants should approach every AI interaction in the course.

**Superagency and Human Value Proposition (HVP)** describe what becomes possible through human + AI partnership, and what makes the human contribution worth amplifying. Superagency is the expanded capability; HVP is the human judgment, context, and accountability that makes the expanded capability valuable. These two concepts always appear together — neither stands alone.

**3Cs (Context, Choices, Confirmation)** is a framework for AI interaction. Provide context so AI understands the situation. Generate choices so the human has options to evaluate. Seek confirmation so decisions are deliberate, not automatic.

**UMPIRE** is a thinking framework for problem navigation — a structured approach to moving through complex, ill-defined problems toward actionable framing.

**DIKW (Data, Information, Knowledge, Wisdom)** describes the hierarchy from raw data to actionable wisdom. The human contribution lives at the upper layers — knowledge and wisdom — where organizational context, professional judgment, and ethical reasoning determine what the data means and what to do about it.

## A note on participant vocabulary

Working professionals do not need to learn these framework names. The frameworks shape course design and instructor language, not necessarily participant vocabulary. Participants should develop the capabilities these frameworks describe. Whether they can name the frameworks is secondary.
