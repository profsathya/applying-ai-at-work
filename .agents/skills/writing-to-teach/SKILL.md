# Writing to Teach

Apply this guidance within the source authority and edit scope in [the authoring contract](../../../docs/AUTHORING.md). Use it to make the intended learning easier, not to impose a page template.

## Begin where the participant is

Establish what working professionals already know, what prior work they have in hand, the capability this material develops, and the evidence they will produce. Use supplied context and relevant earlier artifacts. Surface a consequential gap rather than inventing a course decision.

Open with the first meaningful action or essential concept, with enough context to make it understandable. Use short, descriptive sections when they help participants find the next move; do not make them pass through repeated purpose or prerequisite panels before starting. A participant may be skimming for the next step, checking why it matters, or exploring the idea. Offer a clear route into the work without labeling participants or speculating about their emotions.

## Explain a connection, then let the participant use it

- Connect familiar work to a new idea. Name the actor, action, and reason a step leads to the next; do not make the reader reconstruct a chain of jargon or fragments.
- Define necessary vocabulary at first use in ordinary language. Preserve useful disciplinary terms once explained.
- Use a concrete example when it resolves a likely ambiguity. Add a non-example when the distinction changes a decision. Explain why it fails, rather than merely calling it wrong.
- Interleave explanation with an observation, choice, or small application when the topic benefits from practice. Do not force every concept page to create a graded submission.
- Keep teaching and task directions distinguishable. Put essential instructions where the participant uses them; offer a specific refresher link for prerequisite detail when available.

For example, before asking someone to identify a workflow bottleneck, connect it to a handoff they know: "A request waits two days for approval even though approval takes ten minutes. The waiting time makes this handoff worth investigating. Find a similar wait in the process you mapped." "Our process is inefficient" is a non-example because it gives no location or evidence to investigate.

## Reduce reading effort without removing the teaching

Use complete, connected prose when it explains cause or meaning. Use lists for steps and parallel choices. Concise copy removes avoidable work for the reader; it does not necessarily have the fewest words.

Remove duplicated framing, unsupported promises, unnecessary warnings, and generic reassurance. Retain a useful return to an idea when it adds a different example, application, or retrieval opportunity. A short page that only says "Analyze the workflow" may need more explanation, not a shorter sentence.

For asynchronous delivery, teach enough for the next meaningful action. Keep each explanation or example only when it supports that action or resolves a consequential distinction. Place required teaching where it is used; do not hide prerequisites in optional help. Defer detail about later work until participants need it. Returning to a concept should add practice, retrieval, or a new decision, rather than another explanation of the same point.

Preserve a supplied dialogue, worked case, question sequence, or other unconventional structure when it serves the design. Headings, examples, and activity counts are contextual choices. For homepage copy, apply only the relevant clarity and brevity guidance; do not add a miniature lesson to every row.

## Organize the page visually

Use a clear reading column and group related content by its instructional job. A restrained card can distinguish a worked example, comparison, or response area; a border around every paragraph creates competing units. Choose section and example counts from the learning sequence, not a fixed template.

Consider a diagram for a process or relationship, a table for a comparison, or a worked example for a consequential judgment. Include the visual when it makes the explanation easier to follow; do not add decoration merely to break up text. Prefer semantic text diagrams when they communicate the idea. Keep the needed reasoning in or next to the visual without repeating its entire explanation below it.

Essential information must remain available as text, in a meaningful mobile reading order, without relying on color alone. Give images appropriate alternative text and explain complex relationships in adjacent text. For outside images, verify permission or license for the intended reuse and provide creator, source link, and license or permission credit. Attribution alone is not reuse permission. Label invented examples as illustrative; do not present generated imagery as documentary evidence.

For supported Markdown, presentation choices, and the approved page example, read [the implementation reference](../../../docs/AUTHORING_PRESENTATION.md) when arranging a page or adding a visual. Keep styling in the renderer; artifact bodies remain Canvas-native Markdown.

Use its Sprint 1 reference set to choose an appropriate page pattern, and its illustration brief when imagery helps. A scene may establish concrete workplace context; a diagram or worked example may explain a relationship or decision. State that purpose before selecting or generating an asset. Reuse suitable course assets with their provenance, and keep a consistent visual family within a module. No image count, step count, or example count is required. A restricted Markdown author returns the visual brief to the coordinator, which owns asset generation and integration within the authorized scope.

## Keep the participant's judgment visible

Use real work and real stakeholders. If AI helps organize observations or propose alternatives, state what evidence the participant checks and what decision remains theirs. An AI-generated stakeholder response cannot replace contact with that stakeholder. Do not introduce AI use when it does not support the intended thinking.

For the final editorial pass, use [reviewing-course-text](../reviewing-course-text/SKILL.md). Report judgments as judgments, not proof that participants learned.

## Evidence informing asynchronous delivery

[IES guidance](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1) supports alternating worked examples with practice and using retrieval; brevity must not remove these learning opportunities. [Quality Matters](https://www.qualitymatters.org/qa-resources/resource-center/articles-resources/bill-of-rights-for-online-learners) emphasizes efficient navigation, sufficient instructions, and materials aligned with learning activities. These support purposeful explanations and clear task placement, not universal word or screen limits.
