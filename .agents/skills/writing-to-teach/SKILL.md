# Writing to Teach

Apply this guidance within the source authority and edit scope in [the authoring contract](../../../docs/AUTHORING.md). Use it to make the intended learning easier, not to impose a page template.

## Begin where the participant is

Establish what working professionals already know, what prior work they have in hand, the capability this material develops, and the evidence they will produce. Use supplied context and relevant earlier artifacts. Surface a consequential gap rather than inventing a course decision.

Make the first meaningful action understandable with the information available at that point. A participant may be skimming for the next step, checking why it matters, or exploring the idea. Offer a clear route into the work without labeling participants or speculating about their emotions.

## Explain a connection, then let the participant use it

- Connect familiar work to a new idea. Name the actor, action, and reason a step leads to the next; do not make the reader reconstruct a chain of jargon or fragments.
- Define necessary vocabulary at first use in ordinary language. Preserve useful disciplinary terms once explained.
- Use a concrete example when it resolves a likely ambiguity. Add a non-example when the distinction changes a decision. Explain why it fails, rather than merely calling it wrong.
- Interleave explanation with an observation, choice, or small application when the topic benefits from practice. Do not force every concept page to create a graded submission.
- Keep teaching and task directions distinguishable. Put essential instructions where the participant uses them; offer a specific refresher link for prerequisite detail when available.

For example, before asking someone to identify a workflow bottleneck, connect it to a handoff they know: "A request waits two days for approval even though approval takes ten minutes. The waiting time makes this handoff worth investigating. Find a similar wait in the process you mapped." "Our process is inefficient" is a non-example because it gives no location or evidence to investigate.

## Reduce reading effort without removing the teaching

Use complete, connected prose when it explains cause or meaning. Use lists for steps and parallel choices. Concise copy removes avoidable work for the reader; it does not necessarily have the fewest words.

Remove duplicated framing, unsupported promises, unnecessary warnings, and generic reassurance. Retain a useful return to an idea when it adds a different example, application, or retrieval opportunity. A short page that only says "Analyze the workflow" may need more explanation, not a shorter sentence.

Preserve a supplied dialogue, worked case, question sequence, or other unconventional structure when it serves the design. Headings, examples, and activity counts are contextual choices. For homepage copy, apply only the relevant clarity and brevity guidance; do not add a miniature lesson to every row.

## Keep the participant's judgment visible

Use real work and real stakeholders. If AI helps organize observations or propose alternatives, state what evidence the participant checks and what decision remains theirs. An AI-generated stakeholder response cannot replace contact with that stakeholder. Do not introduce AI use when it does not support the intended thinking.

For the final editorial pass, use [reviewing-course-text](../reviewing-course-text/SKILL.md). Report judgments as judgments, not proof that participants learned.
