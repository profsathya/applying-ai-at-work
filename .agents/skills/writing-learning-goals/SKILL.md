# Writing Learning Goals

Follow [the authoring contract](../../../docs/AUTHORING.md). Treat a goal as a credible capability with a reason to develop it, not a list of topics or required clicks.

## State a capability and its value

- Name what the participant will be able to do in language understandable before the lesson. Explain why that capability matters in their work.
- Promise something the participant can demonstrate. Do not promise an employer's response, guaranteed efficiency gains, or mastery beyond the available practice.
- Frame the value as a capability or opportunity the participant gains, rather than fear, scarcity, or professional survival. For example, "Compare AI suggestions against your evidence so you can choose a useful next step" gives a credible payoff; "Learn AI so you do not become obsolete" uses an unsupported threat.
- Prefer a recognizable skill to an inventory of methods. "Complete an interview worksheet" describes an activity; "Use stakeholder evidence to distinguish a recurring problem from an isolated complaint, so you can choose a problem worth investigating" describes a capability and value.
- Split a goal when it combines independent capabilities with different evidence. Keep connected steps together when they serve one meaningful capability. Do not prescribe a fixed number of goals.

## Connect goals to practice and evidence

For each relevant goal, trace:

| Relationship | Question |
|---|---|
| Prior work | What evidence or skill does the participant already bring? |
| Practice | What will they actually do to develop this capability? |
| Evidence | What observable work or reasoning would demonstrate it? |
| Criteria | What makes that evidence sufficient or strong in the supplied design? |

Use existing tasks and assessment expectations as sources. Evidence may be a work product, an explained decision, or an observation; it need not become a new graded deliverable. For example, a stakeholder interview goal cannot be demonstrated by an AI-written imaginary interview. A justified choice using notes from an actual conversation may provide evidence if the design calls for that choice.

When planning, check the supplied design's relationships and surface gaps. Do not replace the design, create assessments, set points, or change the module budget to make the map look complete. A missing grading policy is a human decision; unclear phrasing around an already specified capability is usually an autonomous copy improvement.

For curated homepage goals, describe what the linked work actually supports. A module that introduces comparison criteria does not yet support a promise to deploy a reliable production system.

Return the relevant goal-to-evidence relationships and unresolved gaps to the parent workflow. Keep this reasoning outside participant content unless it helps the participant understand the work.
