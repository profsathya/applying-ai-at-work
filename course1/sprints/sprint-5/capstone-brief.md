# Capstone Brief

Week 10 has three deliverables. This page explains what they are, how they connect, and what success looks like. Read it before you start any of the three.

## What this week is

This is a competence moment, not a test. You are not being asked to recall concepts or demonstrate familiarity with terminology. You are showing what you can now do: take a real workplace problem, frame it rigorously, validate it with the people it affects, and account for the judgment calls you made along the way.

In Week 1 you had a concern, maybe a vague one. By now you have a validated frame: a specific, scoped problem with identified stakeholders, grounded assumptions, and a documented history of how your thinking changed in response to actual conversations. That is the work. The capstone makes it visible.

## The three deliverables

### 1. Capstone Demonstration

You will produce a short demonstration of your validated problem frame. The format is your choice:

- A written walk-through (document or slide deck with written narration)
- A recorded presentation (screen capture, slides with voiceover, or direct-to-camera)
- A slide deck with speaker notes substantial enough to stand alone

The format does not affect how you are evaluated. Choose the one that fits your communication style and your stakeholders. Some participants will have stakeholders who read documents; others work with people who prefer a short video. Pick accordingly.

Your demonstration is shared with two audiences: your peer cohort and at least one stakeholder from your stakeholder map. The stakeholder does not need to attend a live session. A recorded video they can watch on their own time, or a document they can read, is fine.

What the demonstration must show: where your frame started, what tested it, and what you changed as a result. A strong demonstration shows a thinking process, not just a conclusion.

### 2. Human Judgment Articulation

Separate from the demonstration, you will submit a written account of the human judgment you brought to this work.

This is the most important thing you produce this week. It is also the thing AI could not produce for you. Your organizational context, your stakeholder relationships, your decisions about what to include and exclude, your calls about what mattered enough to change your frame -- none of that came from AI. This deliverable asks you to name it explicitly.

The articulation is short: a few hundred words. The quality standard is specificity. "I applied my judgment" is not sufficient. "I knew from prior experience that the operations team would push back on timeline assumptions, so I scoped the problem to exclude launch sequencing" is.

### 3. Closing Cohort Reflection

The final deliverable is a structured discussion post to your cohort. You will describe the most significant change in how you frame problems -- comparing how you would have approached your chosen problem in Week 1 versus how you approach it now. You will also respond to at least two peers.

This is not a celebration post. It is a professional accounting of what shifted, grounded in specific examples from your own sprint work.

## How these three connect

The demonstration shows your stakeholders and peers what you built. The human judgment articulation shows your instructor what you brought to the build that AI could not. The closing reflection shows your cohort what changed for you across the full arc. Together they document what you can now do.

## A note on the stakeholder audience

At least one person from your stakeholder map should see your demonstration. This is not a formality. Your problem frame was built on real conversations with real people in your organization. Closing the loop with even one of them -- showing them what you did with what they told you -- is the most professional thing you can do at the end of this process.

You do not need their sign-off. You do not need them to attend anything. You need to share the work with them.
