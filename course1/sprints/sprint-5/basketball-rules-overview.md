# Basketball Rules Overview

Basketball is a team game built around a simple objective: score more points than the other team before the game ends. A team scores by putting the ball through the opponent’s basket, and it prevents points by defending legally, rebounding missed shots, and forcing turnovers.

This overview uses the official NBA rulebook as its guide. Local, school, youth, and recreational leagues may adjust timing, court size, foul limits, or substitution rules, but the core ideas below will help you understand the game when you watch or play.

## Teams and Players

Each team has five players on the court. Teams may substitute players during legal stoppages, and NBA players are disqualified after their sixth personal foul. For a beginner, the main takeaway is that basketball is played five against five, with teammates sharing responsibility for ball movement, spacing, defense, and rebounding.

The common positions are guard, forward, and center. Modern basketball is flexible, so do not treat those labels as rigid job descriptions. Watch what each player is doing:

  * Guards often handle the ball, organize the offense, and defend on the perimeter.
  * Forwards often combine scoring, passing, rebounding, and defense.
  * Centers often protect the basket, rebound, screen, and finish near the rim.



## Scoring

NBA scoring uses three basic point values:

  * A free throw is worth 1 point.
  * A field goal from on or inside the three-point line is worth 2 points.
  * A field goal from outside the three-point line is worth 3 points.



A made basket counts when the ball enters the basket from above and goes through or remains in the net. A player’s foot position matters on three-point attempts. The shooter must be outside the three-point line before the shot is released, and cannot be touching the line or the two-point area at that moment.

## Game Timing

An NBA game has four 12-minute regulation periods. If the score is tied after the fourth period, the game continues with 5-minute overtime periods until there is a winner.

The game clock does not simply run nonstop. It stops when officials whistle a foul, violation, timeout, or other stoppage. It also has special stopping rules late in periods. For a beginner, the important habit is to watch both the score and the clock. Late-game strategy changes quickly because every possession matters more when little time remains.

## Moving the Ball

The offense can move the ball by dribbling or passing.

Dribbling means bouncing the ball while moving or standing. A player may not run with the ball without dribbling. After a player voluntarily ends a dribble, that player may pass or shoot, but may not start a second dribble unless the ball was touched by an opponent or another legal exception applies. A player also cannot carry the ball by placing a hand under it, pausing it, and then continuing the dribble.

Passing moves the ball faster than dribbling and helps create open shots. A legal pass can go to any teammate who is inbounds, but the offense must avoid throwing the ball out of bounds, into the backcourt illegally, or into a defender who can steal it.

Shooting is an attempt to score. Good beginners’ shooting habits include setting the feet, facing the basket, using the legs for balance, following through, and getting back on defense after the shot. Whether a shot is worth 2 or 3 points depends on where the shooter is when the ball is released.

## Out of Bounds and Throw-Ins

A player is out of bounds when touching the floor or an object on or outside a boundary line. The ball is out of bounds when it touches a player who is out of bounds, the floor or an object on or outside a boundary, the supports or back of the backboard, or certain areas behind the backboard.

In most out-of-bounds situations, the team that touched the ball last loses possession. The other team restarts play with a throw-in from the boundary near where the ball went out. The thrower usually has 5 seconds to release the ball. After a made field goal or certain made free throws, the team that was scored on puts the ball back in play from the endline.

## Common Violations

A violation is a rule break that usually gives the ball to the other team. Common examples include:

  * Traveling: moving with the ball without dribbling.
  * Double dribble: ending a dribble, then starting another one without a legal interruption.
  * Carrying: putting a hand under the ball, pausing it, and continuing the dribble.
  * Out of bounds: touching the ball last before it goes out.
  * Five-second throw-in: failing to inbound the ball within the allowed time.
  * Intentional kick or punch: deliberately moving the ball with the leg or striking it with a fist.
  * Offensive three seconds: staying too long in the lane while your team controls the ball in the frontcourt.
  * Defensive three seconds: staying in the lane without actively guarding an opponent.
  * Eight seconds: failing to advance the ball from the backcourt to the frontcourt in time.
  * Backcourt violation: causing the ball to go from the frontcourt to the backcourt and being the first team to touch it.
  * Shot clock violation: failing to attempt a legal field goal before the shot clock expires.



Violations matter because they often stop a promising possession before a shot is taken.

## Fouls and Free Throws

A foul is illegal contact or unsportsmanlike conduct. Personal fouls include many forms of illegal contact, such as blocking, holding, pushing, charging, or hitting a shooter. Offensive fouls are committed by the team with the ball. Defensive fouls are committed by the team trying to stop the offense.

Free throws are uncontested shots from the free throw line. They are awarded in several situations, including many shooting fouls, technical fouls, flagrant fouls, and team-foul penalty situations. In NBA regulation periods, the first four common team fouls in a period generally lead to possession for the offended team, while additional common fouls put the offended team in the penalty and can lead to free throws.

Technical and flagrant fouls address behavior or contact that goes beyond ordinary play. These calls can carry free throws, possession consequences, ejections, or later discipline.

## Shot Clock

The shot clock limits how long a team can possess the ball before attempting a field goal. In the NBA, it normally starts at 24 seconds when a team gains new possession. To avoid a shot clock violation, the ball must leave the shooter’s hand before the clock expires and then touch the rim.

The shot clock encourages active play. Offenses cannot hold the ball indefinitely, and defenses can earn a stop by forcing a rushed or late shot.

## Sportsmanship

Good basketball requires contact, speed, and emotion, so sportsmanship matters. Play hard without trying to injure anyone. Respect officials, teammates, opponents, and the people keeping score or managing the game. Help reset the ball promptly after stoppages. If you commit a foul, acknowledge the call and move on.

When watching a game, notice how often the best players compete intensely while still staying composed. That balance is part of understanding basketball, not separate from it.
