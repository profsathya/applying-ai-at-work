# General Rules for Basketball

Basketball is played by two teams trying to score by putting the ball through the opponent’s basket. A standard game is built around possession: one team has the ball and tries to create a good shot while the other team defends, contests, rebounds, and tries to force a turnover.

Rules vary by level of play, but the basic structure is consistent across most organized basketball.

## Teams and positions

Each team has five players on the court at a time. Traditional position names are point guard, shooting guard, small forward, power forward, and center. Modern teams often use more flexible roles, but the basic responsibilities still matter:

  * Guards usually handle the ball, start the offense, defend on the perimeter, and create passing angles.
  * Forwards often combine scoring, rebounding, cutting, screening, and versatile defense.
  * Centers usually protect the rim, rebound, screen, finish near the basket, and anchor interior defense.



Teams can substitute players during dead-ball situations. Coaches use substitutions to manage fatigue, matchups, foul trouble, and offensive or defensive needs.

## Scoring

There are three common scoring values:

  * A free throw is worth 1 point.
  * A shot made inside the three-point line is worth 2 points.
  * A shot made behind the three-point line is worth 3 points.



A field goal is any made basket from live play. A player who is fouled while shooting may receive free throws, depending on the situation.

## Timing

Basketball is divided into periods. In the NBA, regulation games have four 12-minute quarters. Other levels use different period lengths, but the basic rhythm is the same: teams play timed periods, stop for breaks, and may play overtime if the score is tied at the end of regulation.

The shot clock limits how long the offense can hold the ball before attempting a shot. If a team does not shoot in time, it commits a shot-clock violation and loses possession.

## Movement with the ball

A player may move with the ball by dribbling. Once a player stops dribbling, they must pass, shoot, or pivot. Common movement violations include:

  * Traveling: moving illegally with the ball without dribbling.
  * Double dribble: dribbling, stopping, and then dribbling again.
  * Carrying or palming: placing the hand under the ball in a way that interrupts a legal dribble.



These rules keep the game balanced. The offense can use skill, timing, and footwork, but cannot simply run with the ball.

## Fouls

A foul is illegal contact or unsportsmanlike conduct. Common personal fouls include holding, pushing, blocking, charging, and illegal contact on a shooter.

Fouls matter because they can:

  * Give the other team free throws.
  * Give the other team possession.
  * Put a player in foul trouble.
  * Change how aggressively a team can defend.



Not every contact is a foul. Basketball is a contact sport, and officials judge whether contact creates an unfair advantage or disrupts legal movement.

## Violations

Violations are rule breaks that usually result in a turnover rather than free throws. Common examples include:

  * Out of bounds.
  * Traveling.
  * Double dribble.
  * Shot-clock violation.
  * Backcourt violation.
  * Three seconds in the lane on offense.



Violations shape the pace of play because they reward spacing, timing, and ball control.

## Defense and possession

The defense tries to stop scoring by contesting shots, cutting off driving lanes, forcing difficult passes, and securing rebounds. A possession usually ends with a made basket, missed shot and rebound, turnover, foul, or period-ending buzzer.

Good basketball is not only about making shots. It is also about choosing which shots to take, moving without the ball, helping teammates, and making the opponent work for every possession.
