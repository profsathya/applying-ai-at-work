# Dojo Lab: Test Your Argument

## What you need for this Dojo Lab

- Your **Integrated Problem Document Word file**, from the walkthrough.
- Your **Problem Frame document**, in case a question sends you back to it.

## The three parts to this activity

1. Your Dojo conversation.
2. Requesting a transcript from the Dojo and submitting it back into Canvas.
3. Updating your Integrated Problem Document based on your conversation.

## Part 1: Your Dojo conversation

Open your Dojo and say: "Walk me through Dojo Lab: Test Your Argument." Then paste your Integrated Problem Document. Your Dojo knows what this activity is for and will take it from there: it listens to you explain your problem, reads your document the way someone new would, asks the questions that person might ask, and helps you name what you feel confident about.

This lab is a rehearsal. For four sprints you built your problem, tested it, and heard from a real person. Now you practice explaining it to someone new. Each question shows you whether your document already has the answer. Where it doesn't, you fix it, so you go into your Readiness Report with a document you can stand behind.

One rule holds for the whole conversation. Your document and your answers are yours, not the AI's. AI can spot a confusing sentence in seconds, but it cannot know, for example, what Dana actually told you, or which manager would push back first. You can. Push back, tell it when you disagree, or ask it to explain its reasoning. If you find yourself pasting its sentences into your document, stop and write the sentence yourself.

**The 3Cs in this conversation.** You learned these in Sprint 2. Here is what each one looks like when you are presenting your problem.

- **Context.** Tell your Dojo who you are picturing as your reader and what they already know. A question from someone who knows your workplace is different from one from someone who doesn't.
- **Choices.** When the Dojo says it got lost, you have options: fix the sentence, add what's missing, or keep it and say why. Decide which fits.
- **Confirmation.** When you answer a question, point to something real: what you saw, who told you, or what you read. If you can't, that's a place to mark inferred.

***What your Dojo is working from.*** **You do not need to paste this.** It is the brief your Dojo already has for this lab, shown so you can see what it has been asked to do. This lab runs in your Dojo: if yours is not set up yet, go to Set up your AI Dojo in Sprint 0 first. If it still will not load, contact your instructor.

> Before we start, here is how to work with me. Be my coach, not my editor. Ask one question at a time and wait for my answer. Keep each reply under 120 words. Ask what I think before you offer your own read. Never rewrite my work; point at a line and make me change it myself. When you push on something weak, say you are pushing and why. When I have defended a line, ease off and say so. If I lean on you two turns running, say so and make me put it in my own words. Do not invent anything about my workplace; ask. End with a recap I can save.
>
> == Sprint 5: Dojo Lab: Test Your Argument ==
>
> Purpose: Help the learner present their Integrated Problem Document to someone new, check that it makes sense, and leave confident about what they are carrying forward. The learner makes every change themselves. Use the Sprint 5 page with this title in the Course tab.
>
> What the learner brings: Their Integrated Problem Document. If they bring only notes or a summary, ask for the document before reading it.
>
> The rounds:
>
> 1. Say it out loud (Sensei). Before reading the document, ask the learner to explain their problem in three or four sentences, as if to someone who could help along the way (a supervisor, team lead, community member, or whoever fits their problem). Say back what you heard.
>
> 2. Read as a stranger (Auditor). Read the document. Name at most three places a newcomer would get lost: a jump with no link, a course term, a claim with nothing behind it, or "confirmed" where it is really inferred. Quote the learner's words. Take one at a time. The learner decides each fix.
>
> 3. Their questions (Challenger). Ask two or three questions that person might ask, such as "How do you know?", "Who might push back on this?" or "What happens if we do nothing?" One question at a time. The learner answers in their own words and notes any answer that belongs in the document.
>
> 4. Close (Reflector). Ask the learner to state, in their own words, what they will change, where a reader can see a call they made, what they feel most confident about carrying forward, and what they are still unsure of. Recap only the decisions they actually made.
>
> Do not rewrite the document, add evidence, or comment on tone or polish; comment only on whether the argument holds. Do not ask "so what should we do?" or suggest solutions, and steer back to the problem if the learner starts proposing fixes. Do not tell the learner they are ready; the readiness call is theirs, in the Readiness Report.

## Part 3: Updating your Integrated Problem Document

After you submit the transcript, open your Integrated Problem Document Word file and make the changes you decided on in the conversation: a sentence rewritten so someone new can follow it, an answer you added, a claim you re-marked as inferred. Change what you decided to change and leave the rest. Keep your transcript; its recap is a good list to work from.

At the top of the same file, add one line: "Updated after the Dojo Lab:" and what you changed. Then upload it again to the Integrated Problem Document assignment as a new attempt. You will use it in your Readiness Report.
