# Integrated Problem Document

Put four sprints of work together into one short document that someone who could help along the way can read and follow. Nothing here is new research. The work is choosing, ordering, and writing it plainly.

**What to bring:** your Problem Frame ("Last updated: Sprint 4"), your Sprint 2 tables (Dig into your assumptions and What solutions already exist), your Stakeholder Map and Stakeholder Validation Report, Name the Gap, your Learning Plan, and your Dojo Lab transcripts from Sprints 1-4.

**Length:** one to two pages, not counting your pasted Problem Frame.

## Section 1: The problem

This is the first thing your reader sees. In three to five plain sentences, tell them:

- **What is happening:** the problem as it stands now, after everything you've tested.
- **Who it affects, and what it costs them:** time, money, errors, or strain.
- **Where things break down today:** the point where the current way of handling it stops working.

Keep it to the problem. Don't suggest a solution, and leave out course terms like "frame" or "assumption."

A quick test: could someone outside the course read it once and explain your problem back to you?

*Sample:* On our account team, when a client account changes hands, the handover happens in a private conversation between the outgoing and incoming manager. Nothing reaches the rest of the team. As a result, other managers may not know who owns an account, and clients may end up repeating their history to someone who should already know it. The current approach works for the two people in the conversation but breaks down the moment anyone else needs to know.

## Section 1: Your current Problem Frame

Under your paragraph, paste your current Problem Frame (Parts 1-7, from your Sprint 4 Learning Plan's updated frame) and tighten the wording where needed. Don't rewrite it.

## Section 2: What I know, and how sure I am

Your reader's next question will be "How do you know?" This section answers it honestly. You aren't expected to have proof. Most of what you know at this point is still inferred, and saying so clearly is a strength.

In two or three short paragraphs:

- **Name the two or three things your problem rests on most.** For example, who it affects, what it costs them, how it's handled today, or what already exists that touches it. If you include what already exists, say what you found when you looked, not whether it's good enough.
- **For each one, say what supports it in plain words.** What did you see, who did you talk to, what did you read? Write "When I talked to Dana...", not "In my Sprint 3 report..."
- **Say how sure you are.** Confirmed means you checked it against something independent, and it held. Inferred means you believe it but haven't checked.
- **End with what you don't know yet,** and why it matters to the problem.

**Where to look:** your Problem Frame (parts 6 and 7), your What solutions already exist table, your Stakeholder Validation Report (Sections B and D), and Name the Gap (A1 and A2).

*Sample:* I know how handovers happen today. The outgoing manager tells the incoming manager directly. I've watched it happen twice, and Dana described it the same way. I believe clients end up repeating their history, but that's still inferred, since I haven't heard it from a client. What I don't know yet is whether the other managers find out late too, and why the shared handover sheet stopped being used. Until I know those, I can't say how widespread this problem is.

## Section 3: How the problem changed, or why it held

Your problem has been tested since Sprint 1. This section shows your reader what that testing did to it. Write about the problem, not about yourself. How your own thinking grew belongs in your Readiness Report.

Pick two or three moments where you tested your problem. For each one, write a few sentences:

- **What you believed before.**
- **What tested it:** a conversation, something you looked into, something you observed, or an AI exchange that pushed back on your thinking. If AI changed your mind, say whether you checked it afterward.
- **What happened:** it changed (and how), or it held (and why you kept it).

Both outcomes are full answers. A problem that held up after real testing is just as strong as one that changed.

**Where to look:** your Problem Frame updates in Sprint 2 (taking stock), Sprint 3 (mid-course revision), and Sprint 4 (Learning Plan), plus your Dojo Lab transcripts from Sprints 1-4.

*Sample:*

- **Changed:** I thought the CRM's owner field would solve this. In the Dojo, AI pushed on that. When I looked, the field already exists. The real issue is that nobody makes people fill it in.
- **Changed:** My frame said nobody had tried to fix handovers before. Then I learned a shared handover sheet once existed and stopped being used, so the problem includes why that attempt faded.
- **Held:** I still believe handovers happen only in a private conversation. I've watched it twice, and Dana described it the same way, so I kept it.

## Section 4: What a solution must do

Your reader will want to know what a good solution to your problem would have to achieve. This section says that, without picking the solution. Choosing and building a solution comes in CIS 502.

**Where to look:** your Problem Frame (part 5, "Fixed looks like"), the Need and Constraints rows on your Stakeholder Map, and your Dojo Lab notes from Sprints 1-4.

**4a: Two to four requirements.** A requirement says what any solution to your problem must do, or must not break. For each one, write a sentence or two:

- **The requirement,** in plain words.
- **Where it comes from:** a part of your Problem Frame, or a person on your Stakeholder Map.
- **How sure you are:** confirmed or inferred.

Check that each requirement doesn't already name a tool or a fix. If it does, it is a solution, not a requirement. "We need a shared tracker" is already a solution. "Everyone knows within a day" leaves it open.

*Sample:* Any solution to late handovers must let everyone on the team know within a day when an account changes hands. This comes from my Problem Frame, but it's inferred, since nobody has said what "fast enough" is. A solution also can't add another step for Dana, the account lead. That comes from her Need on my Stakeholder Map, and it's inferred too, because it's my read of her, not something she said.

## Section 4b: One conflict

Name one place where two requirements, or two people, pull in different directions, and say what that means for any solution. If you didn't find one, say so and where one might show up. In the Dojo Lab that follows, the "Who might push back?" question will test your conflict. You decide whether to change it.

*Sample:* These two pull against each other. Dana thinks the handover process works fine, while the managers say clients keep repeating their history. Any solution has to close the gap for the managers without feeling like extra work to Dana.
