# Sprint 3 Briefing

This sprint runs two weeks. By the end of it, you will have three things:

1. **An AI fit analysis** - a clear-eyed answer to whether AI actually belongs in your solution, and why.
2. **Concrete success criteria** - specific, testable conditions that would tell you the problem is solved.
3. **A human-vs-AI contribution map** - a written breakdown of what AI can do in this situation and what only you can do.

## The question this sprint forces

You have been framing a problem. Now it is time to ask a harder question: does AI actually help here, or are you forcing it in?

This is not a rhetorical question. For some problems, AI is genuinely useful - it can process volume, surface patterns, draft outputs, or reduce time on tasks that eat up capacity. For other problems, AI is the wrong tool. The problem may require trust, relationship, authority, or judgment that is context-specific to your organization. Wrapping AI around it adds complexity without adding value.

If AI does not belong in your solution, the right answer is to say so. That is not a failure. It is the kind of honest assessment that distinguishes capable professionals from ones who follow trends. This sprint gives you explicit permission to conclude that AI is not a fit - and asks you to make the case either way.

## What success criteria are and why they matter

A success criterion is a specific, observable condition that would tell you the problem is solved. Not "people are happier" or "the process is more efficient." Something like: "The team lead spends less than 30 minutes per week on X" or "New staff complete onboarding without needing a follow-up session."

Success criteria do two things. First, they force precision - you cannot write a measurable criterion for a vague problem. If you are struggling to write the criteria, the problem statement is still underspecified. Second, they give you something to validate. When you take a criterion to a stakeholder and they push back, you learn something real about whether your framing matches what they actually need.

## Your three deliverables

**AI fit analysis.** A short written assessment - a paragraph or two - of whether AI adds genuine value to your problem. Identify what, specifically, AI could do: pattern recognition, drafting, classification, retrieval, something else. Then identify what AI cannot do: organizational judgment, stakeholder trust, authority, accountability. State your conclusion. If AI belongs in the solution, explain how. If it does not, explain that instead.

**Success criteria.** Two to four specific, testable criteria that define what solved looks like for your problem. Each criterion should be concrete enough that you could point to evidence that it has been met or has not been met.

**Human-vs-AI contribution map.** A structured breakdown - a short table or two-column list - with two columns: what AI contributes, and what you contribute. Be honest about both sides. The human column is not a formality. It should name real things: your knowledge of the people involved, your judgment about what the organization will actually adopt, your authority to move something forward.

## Pacing

Week 6: Do the AI fit analysis first. Use your problem statement, scope statement, and stakeholder notes from Sprints 1 and 2. Draft your success criteria. These two pieces inform each other - if you cannot write criteria, your fit analysis may be pointing at a problem that is still too vague.

Week 7: Take your success criteria to a stakeholder. Not to get approval - to find out whether your criteria match what they would actually use to judge success. Adjust based on what you hear. Write your contribution map after that conversation, not before it. Submit all three deliverables before the week ends.
