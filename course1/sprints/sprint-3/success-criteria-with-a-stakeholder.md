# Success Criteria with a Stakeholder

Before you decide how AI fits into your work, you need to know what success actually looks like. "Better," "more efficient," and "higher quality" are not success criteria. They are hopes. This assignment turns those hopes into something testable, then runs them past the person who matters most: someone with real authority over whether your work is considered successful.

## What to do

**Step 1: Draft your success criteria**

Write 3 to 5 criteria that define what success looks like for your problem. Each criterion must be specific enough that a reasonable observer could tell you whether it has been met.

Use this test for each criterion: if you showed it to a skeptical colleague, would they be able to say "yes, that happened" or "no, that did not happen" without asking you to interpret it? If not, it is still aspirational. Sharpen it.

Examples of the difference:

- Aspirational: "The process is faster."
- Specific: "The team completes intake review in under 48 hours, down from the current 5-day average."

- Aspirational: "Stakeholders are more confident in the data."
- Specific: "The department director signs off on the monthly report without requesting revisions."

**Step 2: Identify the right stakeholder**

Choose someone who has actual authority over whether this work is considered successful. This is not necessarily your direct manager. It is the person whose judgment would define whether the outcome is good enough. That might be a budget owner, a department head, a key client, a cross-functional partner, or someone who currently lives with the problem every day.

You will ask for 20 to 30 minutes of their time.

**Step 3: Hold the conversation**

Share your draft criteria with the stakeholder. Your goal is not to present and defend them. Your goal is to find out where they would push back, what you missed, and what they actually care about.

Useful questions to ask:
- "If we hit these criteria six months from now, would you consider this a success? What would make you hesitate?"
- "Is there anything not on this list that you would consider a dealbreaker?"
- "What does failure look like to you for this kind of work?"

Take notes during or immediately after the conversation. Capture what they said, not what you wished they had said.

**Step 4: Revise your criteria**

Rewrite your success criteria based on what you heard. Note what changed and why. If the stakeholder confirmed something you already had, say so. If they redirected you, show that clearly.

## What to submit

Upload a single document (PDF or Word) containing all three parts:

1. **Draft criteria** - Your original 3 to 5 criteria, written before the conversation
2. **Conversation notes** - The stakeholder's name and role, the date, and your notes from the conversation (bullet points are fine)
3. **Revised criteria** - Your updated criteria, with a brief note after each one explaining what changed and what prompted the change

There is no required length. A focused two-page document is better than a padded five-page one.

## How this connects to the rest of Sprint 3

Your revised success criteria are the foundation for the Human-AI Contribution Map you will complete later this sprint. Once you know what success actually requires, you can make a clearer call about where AI can contribute and where it cannot. Criteria that are vague make that call impossible. Criteria that have survived a real stakeholder conversation make it much easier.
