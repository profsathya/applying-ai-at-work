# Sprint 4: Validated Framing

Three sprints in, you have done the hard work: you surfaced a real problem, scoped it to something actionable, identified who it affects, and tested whether AI belongs in the solution. You have written drafts, revised them, and heard from colleagues who pushed back on your thinking.

This sprint pulls all of that together.

The goal is not to produce more drafts. It is to arrive at a complete, integrated problem frame - one that can stand on its own in front of a decision-maker who did not watch you build it. That is a different standard than the one you have been working against. A decision-maker asks: Is this the right problem? Is this the right scope? Do the proposed success criteria reflect what the organization actually needs? They do not give partial credit for effort.

This sprint runs two weeks. By the end of it, you will have three things:

1. **A complete problem frame** - scope, stakeholders, AI fit assessment, and success criteria unified into a single coherent document. Not four separate pieces. One frame.
2. **A validation conversation with a decision-maker** - a scheduled, structured exchange with someone in your organization who has standing to act on the problem. They will tell you what holds and what does not.
3. **A revised frame** - updated based on what you heard. The revision is the evidence that the conversation happened and that you were listening.

## What integration actually means

You have four components developed across three sprints. They were built in sequence, which means they may not yet speak to each other cleanly. Integration means checking for consistency: Does your scope still match your stakeholder list? Does your AI fit assessment still hold given the success criteria you landed on? Does everything point at the same underlying problem?

If the answer to any of those questions is no, fix it before the validation conversation. A decision-maker will notice the inconsistency. You should find it first.

## The decision-maker conversation

The stakeholders you have spoken with so far were chosen because they are affected by the problem. That is the right criterion for understanding the problem. It is not the same as the criterion for choosing someone to validate the frame.

A decision-maker is someone who could act on this problem - approve a project, allocate resources, remove an obstacle, or sponsor the work. They may not be deeply familiar with the day-to-day details. That is fine. Your job in the conversation is to present the frame clearly enough that they can evaluate it on its merits.

Schedule this conversation in Week 8. Come prepared with your integrated frame and three specific questions you want their input on. Listen for what surprises them. That is where your frame is incomplete or where your assumptions about the problem do not match how decision-makers see it.

## Pacing

Week 8: Integrate your four components into a single problem frame. Check for internal consistency. Identify where the seams still show and resolve them. Schedule and conduct your validation conversation with a decision-maker.

Week 9: Revise your frame based on the conversation. Document what changed and why. Submit your complete revised frame along with a brief account of the validation conversation and the revisions it produced.
