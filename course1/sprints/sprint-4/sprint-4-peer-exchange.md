# Sprint 4 Peer Exchange

This is the last peer exchange before the capstone. You have taken your integrated problem frame to a real decision-maker, heard how they read it, and revised it based on what you learned. This discussion captures what that process actually produced - not as a summary of your work, but as a concrete report of the moment that changed something.

Your cohort will see these frames again in Week 10. What you share here is a preview of what you are bringing into the capstone.

## Your initial post

Post two things, clearly labeled:

**1. The single most important thing your decision-maker said.**

Not a paraphrase of the conversation. Not the part that was most encouraging. The one thing they said that told you something you did not already know - about how the problem is seen at their level, where the real constraints are, or what your frame was missing.

Be precise. "They validated my framing" is not the post. "They pointed out that the department head and the operations team report through different chains, which means any solution that touches both groups requires a governance decision I had not included in my scope" is the post.

If everything they said confirmed what you already knew, say that - and explain whether you think the conversation surfaced genuine agreement or just did not create enough friction.

**2. How your frame changed - or why it did not.**

Name the specific revision you made as a result of what you heard. If you narrowed the scope, say what you narrowed it to and what fell out. If you revised the problem statement, say what changed and what drove the change. If you added a stakeholder perspective you had overlooked, say whose perspective and why it matters.

If the frame did not change, do not skip this section. Explain what you heard that bore on your framing and why you held the line. A frame that survived real scrutiny is worth explaining.

Post before reading your peers' responses. Four to eight sentences is the right length.

## Responding to peers

Reply to at least two peers. Your response should do one of two things - pick the one that is most honest given what you read:

**Option A: Name what their decision-maker's feedback reveals about their problem.**

Sometimes the most important information in a validation conversation is not what changes in the frame, but what the pushback tells you about how the problem sits in the organization. If you read your peer's post and notice something about what their decision-maker said - an organizational dynamic, a constraint, a way the problem is politically positioned - that their revised frame does not fully account for, name it.

**Option B: Surface a tension between what they heard and how they revised.**

If the revision your peer describes does not seem to fully follow from the feedback they received, say so. Not to question their judgment, but to push on the reasoning: why does that feedback lead to that change? A useful response completes this sentence: "What your decision-maker said suggests... but your revision does..." and then asks the question that follows from that gap.

Your reply should be three to six sentences. Do not offer general encouragement. Find the place in their post where the reasoning is thinnest and put pressure there.
