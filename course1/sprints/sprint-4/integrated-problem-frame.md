# Integrated Problem Frame

This is the Sprint 4 deliverable. You have spent eight weeks building the components: a problem statement, stakeholder perspectives, scope decisions, success criteria, and a map of what you and AI each contribute. Now you write them as one document.

The goal is not to paste your prior work together. It is to write a single, coherent argument that a reader who does not know your organization can follow from start to finish. If that reader can understand the problem, why it matters, who it affects, what solved looks like, and where human judgment is irreplaceable, then your frame holds up. If they need you to explain it, it does not.

AI is a legitimate drafting partner for this document. Use it if it helps you organize, clarify, or sharpen your writing. What you cannot delegate is the thinking behind the choices. Every section should reflect decisions you have made and can defend in a conversation with a decision-maker.

## The five elements

Your integrated frame must include all five sections below, in any order that makes the argument flow clearly.

---

### 1. Problem statement

State the problem in precise, scoped terms. A good problem statement tells the reader:

- What is happening (or not happening) that should not be (or should be)
- Who is affected and in what way
- Why this matters now, in your organizational context
- What is inside scope and what is outside it

This replaces your Sprint 1 first-pass statement. It should reflect everything you have learned since then: the stakeholder conversations, the scope revisions, the tension between how you originally framed the problem and how it looks after testing. If your Sprint 1 statement still holds, say so and say why. If it changed, show what changed.

One test: could someone from outside your organization read this statement and explain the problem back to you accurately? If not, keep tightening.

---

### 2. Stakeholder perspectives

Identify who is affected by this problem and what each stakeholder's relationship to it is. This is not a list of names. It is a synthesis of what you actually learned from your conversations.

For each stakeholder or stakeholder group, write:

- Their role and relationship to the problem
- What they need from a solution (not what you assume they need - what they told you or what your interactions revealed)
- Any tension or disagreement between stakeholder perspectives that affects how the problem should be framed

If two stakeholders see the problem differently, do not smooth that over. Name it. How you handle that tension is a framing decision, and framing decisions are where your judgment shows.

---

### 3. Scope

Describe the boundaries of your problem frame. What is included? What is explicitly not included, and why? Where is the frame genuinely uncertain?

A scope section that excludes nothing is not a scope section. If everything is in scope, you are describing a situation, not a framed problem.

Be specific about the time horizon, the organizational unit, and any dependencies or assumptions the frame relies on.

---

### 4. Success criteria

State the 3 to 5 criteria that would tell you the problem is solved. These should be the criteria you finalized with your stakeholder in Sprint 3. If you have revised them since that conversation, note what changed and why.

Each criterion must be specific enough that a reasonable observer could tell you whether it has been met, without asking you to interpret it. If a criterion requires you to explain what it means, it is still aspirational.

Check for consistency with your problem statement. If your success criteria would be satisfied while the problem statement is still true, something does not connect. Fix the gap here.

---

### 5. Human and AI contribution split

Draw on your Sprint 3 contribution map. In this context, write one to two paragraphs that explain:

- Where AI contributes to this problem and what specifically it does
- What you and your stakeholders must contribute - the judgment, relationships, and accountability that AI cannot provide for this problem in your organization
- Where the boundary is clearest and where it remains ambiguous

This section should be specific to your problem. Generic statements about AI capabilities or human creativity do not belong here.

---

## On using AI to write this document

You may use AI to draft, organize, and refine sections of this document. If you do, read what it produces and revise it until every sentence reflects your actual thinking - not a plausible version of it.

A useful check: could you explain the reasoning behind any sentence in this document if a decision-maker asked? If a sentence came from AI and you are not sure why it is there, cut it or rewrite it until you are.

The professional judgment visible in this document should be yours. AI can help you express it clearly. It cannot supply it.

## What to submit

Submit as text entry. Write the five sections directly in the submission box. Use the section headings above or your own, as long as the five elements are clearly present and labeled.

Target length is two to three pages (roughly 600 to 900 words). A focused document that covers the five elements clearly is better than a longer one that pads them.

## How this will be evaluated

Your submission is evaluated on three criteria:

- **Completeness and consistency (15 points):** All five elements are present. They speak to each other: the problem statement, stakeholders, scope, success criteria, and contribution split form a coherent argument rather than five separate answers to five separate questions.
- **Written for an outside reader (10 points):** A reader who does not know your organization can follow the frame from start to finish without additional explanation from you.
- **Visible professional judgment (10 points):** The framing choices in this document reflect your decisions and your reasoning. AI phrasing that could apply to any problem in any organization will not satisfy this criterion.
