# Validation Conversation with a Decision-Maker

Your integrated problem frame has been written for a reader who does not know your organization. Now take it to someone in your organization who has authority over whether this problem gets worked on.

This is not a presentation. It is a test.

You are looking for the places where your frame is wrong, incomplete, or out of step with how decision-makers actually see the situation. The goal is to leave the conversation with specific feedback you can act on - not with confirmation that your frame is good.

## Who qualifies as a decision-maker

The person you speak with should have genuine standing on this problem. That means they could:

- Approve a project to address it
- Allocate budget or staff time toward it
- Prioritize it against competing concerns
- Remove an organizational obstacle that affects it

This is typically a manager, director, department head, or client lead - someone above the stakeholders you have already spoken with. It should not be a peer, a colleague at your level, or someone who is primarily affected by the problem rather than positioned to act on it.

If you are uncertain whether someone qualifies, ask yourself: if this person told you the problem was not worth pursuing, would that carry weight? If yes, they qualify. If you would question their authority to make that call, find someone else.

## How to conduct the conversation

**Before the meeting:**

- Share your integrated problem frame in advance so they can read it. Do not walk them through it slide by slide. Give them the document, give them time, then talk.
- Prepare three specific questions where you want their input. Focus on the places you are least confident in your frame.
- Tell them explicitly that you want honest pushback, not encouragement. Most decision-makers will default to supportive unless you signal that criticism is the point.

**During the conversation:**

- Listen more than you explain. If they ask a clarifying question, resist the urge to fill in the gap verbally. That gap is information: your frame did not make that point clearly enough.
- When they push back, do not defend your framing immediately. Ask them to say more. Understand their objection fully before you respond.
- Take notes in real time or immediately after. Memory of what someone said degrades fast, and paraphrase is not the same as what they actually told you.

**After the conversation:**

- Write up your notes while the details are fresh.
- Identify the specific pieces of feedback that require changes to your frame. Separate these from general encouragement or comments that did not surface an actual problem.
- Make the revisions.

## What to submit

Upload a single PDF or document that contains three clearly labeled sections:

**Section 1: The frame as shared**

Include the full text of your integrated problem frame as it existed when you shared it with the decision-maker. Do not revise it before including it here. This is the version they read.

**Section 2: Conversation notes**

Summarize the conversation. Include:

- Who you spoke with and their role (title and relationship to this problem)
- What you asked them and what they said
- The specific points where they pushed back, disagreed, questioned your framing, or raised concerns you had not considered
- Any moments where their response surprised you

Your notes should be detailed enough that someone reading them could understand what happened in the conversation - not just the conclusions, but the exchange. "They thought the scope was too broad" is a conclusion. "They asked whether the problem statement applied to contractors as well as full-time staff, which we had not considered" is what happened.

**Section 3: Revisions**

List the specific changes you are making to your frame as a result of the conversation. For each change:

- State what the original framing said
- State what the feedback was
- State what the revised framing says

A useful format is a short table or numbered list. You do not need to rewrite the entire frame here - just document the changes and the reasoning behind each one.

## A note on conversations that go smoothly

If the decision-maker agreed with everything and had no substantive pushback, that is a result worth scrutinizing. Either your frame is genuinely solid - which is possible - or the conversation did not create enough friction to surface the real concerns.

Before concluding the conversation went smoothly, ask at least one of these questions directly:

- "What would you change about how I have framed this problem?"
- "Where do you think my scope is wrong?"
- "What am I missing that would change how you see this?"

If you ask those questions and still hear only agreement, document that in your notes. But you should be able to explain why you believe the frame is already right, not just report that the decision-maker said so.

## How this will be evaluated

- **Appropriate decision-maker (10 points):** The person you spoke with has genuine authority over whether this problem gets prioritized. Their role and relationship to the problem must be clear in your notes.
- **Pushback documented (10 points):** Your conversation notes show specific points of disagreement, concern, or challenge - not just what you said and what they affirmed. A conversation where the decision-maker raised no concerns will need to show evidence that you pushed for critical feedback.
- **Feedback-driven changes identified (10 points):** Section 3 names specific revisions to your frame that trace directly to the conversation. "I will think more carefully about scope" does not meet this criterion. "I revised the scope to exclude contractors after the decision-maker pointed out our current data does not cover them" does.
