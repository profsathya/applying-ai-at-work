# Shallow-Water Setup And Technique

A good underwater weaving session is mostly preparation. The right setup lets you focus on the weave instead of chasing materials, losing the pattern, or working in a position that becomes unsafe or uncomfortable.

Use this page as a setup checklist and a technique guide for your first basket.

## Choose a controlled water space

Use a shallow, stable, controlled space where you can keep your head above water at all times. Good options include:

- A wide plastic tub on a sturdy table.
- A shallow pool step where you can sit or kneel comfortably.
- A basin set inside a sink or outdoor work area.

Avoid open water, moving water, deep pools, slippery edges, and crowded areas. If you are working in a pool, have another person nearby who knows what you are doing. Keep the session short enough that fatigue does not affect your judgment.

## Prepare the work area

Set up before your hands are wet. You should be able to reach every item without stretching, standing suddenly, or turning away from the basket.

Recommended materials:

- Soaked basket reed or another beginner-safe weaving material.
- A small prepared base or base spokes.
- A shallow tray or mesh pouch for loose reed.
- Towel for your hands and finished basket.
- Blunt scissors kept outside the water.
- Measuring tape or ruler kept outside the water.
- A simple pattern note printed or placed in a waterproof sleeve.

Keep sharp tools out of the water. Trim reed before it goes into the basin or after you remove the basket from the water. Do not cut while your hands are submerged.

## Stage the reed

Sort reed by length and purpose before you begin. Put base spokes, working reed, and rim pieces in separate bundles. If every strip looks the same underwater, tie a small loose marker around one bundle or keep it in a separate tray.

Before each reed enters the basket, check three things:

1. **Flex.** It bends without cracking.
2. **Surface.** It is not fuzzy, slimy, or split.
3. **Length.** It is long enough for the next section, with extra for tucking.

If a reed becomes too stiff while you work, return it to the water briefly. If it becomes too soft, set it aside and use a fresher strip.

## Hold the basket without crushing it

Your grip should stabilize the basket, not clamp it. Hold the base lightly between your fingers and use your thumb to guide the working reed. When the basket sides begin to rise, rotate the basket instead of twisting your wrist around it.

Use this rhythm:

1. Place the reed.
2. Seat it against the previous row.
3. Check the spoke spacing.
4. Rotate the basket.
5. Repeat.

If the basket starts to drift, bring it back to the center of your workspace before placing the next pass. Do not keep weaving while the base is angled or floating away from your hands. That is how uneven sides begin.

## Manage tension

Tension is the main skill. Too loose, and the basket will gap as it dries. Too tight, and the spokes will lean, pinch, or snap.

Look for these signs:

| What you see | Likely cause | Next move |
|---|---|---|
| Wide gaps between rows | Reed is too loose or not seated | Press the row down gently after each round |
| Spokes leaning inward | Reed is pulled too tight | Loosen the last few passes and reset spacing |
| Reed twisting | Hands are rotating the reed as it passes | Flatten the reed before the next spoke |
| One side rising faster | Basket is not rotating evenly | Pause, level the basket, and inspect the last round |
| Split reed | Material is dry, damaged, or overworked | Replace the reed before continuing |

Make one correction at a time. If you change tension, spoke spacing, and reed size all at once, you will not know which change helped.

## Finish and document the session

At the end of the session, rinse loose fibers from the basket, shape it gently, and place it on a towel to dry. Take a photo from the top and a photo from the side while it is still wet. Take the same photos after it dries.

Write down:

- Where you practiced and how the setup worked.
- What material you used.
- What pattern you followed.
- Where the weave became difficult.
- What changed during drying.
- One adjustment for the next attempt.

These notes are part of the craft. They turn a first basket from a one-time activity into a repeatable process.
