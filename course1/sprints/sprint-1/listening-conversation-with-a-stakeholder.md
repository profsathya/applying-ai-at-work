## Listening Conversation with a Stakeholder

This week you are going to talk to someone.

You identified at least one stakeholder when you wrote your first-pass problem statement. This assignment asks you to sit down with one of them for 20 to 30 minutes and listen. Not pitch. Not validate your framing. Listen to how they see the problem.

The conversation you are about to have is the first external test of the framing you built in Week 2. Your first-pass statement reflects what you think is happening. Your stakeholder may see something different. That difference is data.

## Who to talk to

Choose someone from your stakeholder map who is affected by the problem - not someone who shares your view of it. Ideally, pick someone whose perspective you are less certain about. A peer who agrees with you will not teach you as much as a person who is closer to the consequences.

This must be a real person in your organization. Do not ask a classmate to role-play a stakeholder. Do not use AI to simulate the conversation. You have actual people to talk to.

## How to run the conversation

Come with a few open questions, not a presentation. Some options to get you started:

- "When you think about [the problem area], what concerns you most?"
- "What would you say is the root cause, from where you sit?"
- "What have you already tried, or seen tried? What happened?"
- "What would 'better' look like to you?"

Let their answers determine where the conversation goes. Your job is to understand their model of the situation, not to correct it or convince them of yours. Resist the urge to explain your framing mid-conversation. Take notes instead.

A photo of handwritten notes is fine. A typed document is fine. A rough transcript is fine. The format does not matter - the capture does.

## What to submit

Upload a single file containing two things:

**1. Your conversation notes.**

These should reflect what the stakeholder actually said. Paraphrase is fine, but the notes should be grounded in their words and perspective, not in your interpretation of what they meant to say. Include the stakeholder's role and your relationship to them (for example: "direct report," "cross-functional partner in Finance," "customer who uses the system daily"). You do not need to include their name.

**2. A one-paragraph debrief.**

After the conversation, write one paragraph covering:

- What surprised you.
- What contradicted, complicated, or changed your first-pass problem statement from Week 2.
- One follow-up question you now want to ask - of this stakeholder, another stakeholder, or yourself.

Keep the debrief honest. If nothing surprised you, say what confirmed your framing and why that confirmation feels trustworthy. But most conversations with real stakeholders produce at least one thing you did not expect. If yours did, name it directly.

## What this is not

This is not a validation exercise. You are not gathering evidence that you were right. You are finding out how someone else sees the problem - and letting that shift your thinking where it should.

This is also not a research interview with a formal protocol. It is a professional conversation with a colleague. You already know how to have those. The only addition here is that you are taking notes and writing a short debrief afterward.

## Evaluation

Your submission will be evaluated on three things:

- Whether you talked to a real, named stakeholder (by role and relationship).
- Whether your notes reflect what they said rather than what you were hoping to hear.
- Whether your debrief names at least one specific surprise or contradiction with your first-pass statement.

Polished notes do not earn more credit than rough ones. What earns full credit is evidence that you were genuinely listening.
