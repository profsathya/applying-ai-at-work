# Dojo Lab: test your assumptions

You're going to use this Dojo Lab to push on what you've written. Bring what you wrote on the last page. This is the first of two Dojo Labs in the sprint, and it is short. AI is good at one thing here: noticing where your read of your own assumptions is too comfortable. It cannot know which answers are likely where you work. You can.

Use your Dojo, or any AI chat you already have. Your Dojo reads your Problem Frame if you added it in setup; otherwise, paste your Problem Frame first, so that it knows what problem it is being a skeptic about. Change names and drop anything sensitive before you paste.

The rhythm is the one from Sprint 1: bring what you wrote, have it tested, decide what to keep. Nothing here rewrites your walk; you do.

## 1. Test the walk

Paste your tables from the last page, then this:

> I am going to paste my walk through the assumptions in my problem frame: for each one, the questions behind it, the different answers that could change my problem, and how risky it is to shutting the problem down. Your job is to be a skeptic who works in this situation, not an editor. Do not rewrite anything and do not suggest solutions. Do not list everything you notice.
>
> Answer in three short labeled parts, in this order, and nothing else.
>
> PUSHBACK: Two places where you would push back on what I said the answers could change, or on a risk I rated too low. One line each, each starting with my words in quotation marks.
>
> ONE QUESTION: The one question someone who works in this situation would ask me before trusting my risk ratings.
>
> WHAT HELPED: One line on what I gave you that helped you answer, and what you would have wanted earlier.
>
> Here is my walk:

Take what lands and fix your walk yourself. If it pushed on a risk rating and the push was fair, change the rating and the reason. If it was wrong about your situation, say so in your last message; disagreeing with a reason is a good answer.

> **Illustrative exchange, handovers.** It pushed on "if it is one person's habit, there is nothing here": one person's habit can still expose a process that fails whenever accounts move. I have changed that line to "then it is smaller, and probably a conversation rather than a nine-week problem." It pushed on "one day is fast enough to matter," which I had rated low risk, as accepting a target before knowing how fast the work needs the information; true, and it does not change the verdict, so it stays low risk with a better reason. Its question was the best line: how would I tell a manager who has not noticed the problem from one who has adapted to it and no longer calls it a problem? I do not know yet, and that is now written against the managers row.
