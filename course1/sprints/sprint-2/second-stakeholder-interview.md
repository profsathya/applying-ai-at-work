## Second Stakeholder Interview

In Sprint 1, you talked to someone affected by your problem. This week, you are going to talk to someone who sees it differently.

Not slightly differently. Differently enough that they might push back on your framing, describe different consequences, or trace the problem to a different cause. The goal of this conversation is not confirmation. It is friction. Productive, clarifying friction.

By Week 5, you have a scope statement and a more developed problem frame than you did in Week 3. That is exactly when the frame becomes dangerous - when it is specific enough to feel settled but has only been tested against one external perspective. This interview is the second test.

## Who to talk to

Choose someone whose role, vantage point, or relationship to the problem is genuinely different from your Sprint 1 contact.

Some ways to think about "different":

- Different level in the organization (frontline vs. management, or the reverse of whoever you spoke to in Sprint 1)
- Different function (if your first contact was inside your team, find someone outside it)
- Different relationship to the consequences (someone who feels the costs versus someone who manages the system that produces them)
- Someone who has tried to address this problem before and has an opinion about why previous efforts fell short

You do not need to find someone who disagrees with you as a matter of personality. You need someone whose position or experience gives them a genuinely different angle on the same problem.

This must be a real conversation with a real person at your organization. Do not simulate it.

## Preparing your interview plan

Prepare five to eight questions in advance. The questions should be specific to your current problem frame and scope, not generic.

AI can be useful here. If you use it, give the tool your problem statement, your scope boundaries, and what you learned from your Sprint 1 conversation. Ask it to help you generate questions that would surface contradictions or blind spots in your current frame. Then edit the output to fit your actual situation and the specific person you are interviewing.

When you submit, include the questions you went in with (whether or not you asked all of them). This is your interview plan.

A few questions worth including in almost any plan:

- "Where do you think this problem actually starts?"
- "Who else is affected by this that I might not be thinking about?"
- "What has already been tried? What happened?"
- "What would you push back on in how I've framed this?"

The last question takes some confidence to ask. Ask it anyway.

## Running the conversation

Your job in this conversation is to understand how this person sees the problem, not to update them on how you see it. Give them your framing at the start if it helps orient the conversation, then get out of its way.

When they say something that contradicts your frame, do not explain why they might be wrong. Ask a follow-up question. The contradiction is what you came for.

Take notes during or immediately after the conversation. A photo of handwritten notes is fine. A typed document is fine. What matters is that the notes reflect what the stakeholder said, not what you expected them to say.

## What to submit

Upload a single file containing two things:

**1. Your interview plan.**

The questions you prepared in advance, in the order you planned to ask them. Include a one-sentence description of who this person is (role, relationship to the problem) and why you chose them as your second contact. You do not need to include their name.

**2. Your interview notes.**

What they actually said. Paraphrase is fine. Direct quotes are better when you can capture them. The notes should be grounded in their words and perspective.

At the end of the notes, write a short paragraph - three to five sentences - covering:

- The most significant thing they said that cuts against your current framing.
- Whether it changes something you wrote in your scope statement, your problem frame, or both.
- One question you want to answer before your Sprint 2 submission is complete.

If nothing they said surprised you or contradicted your frame, say so directly - and then explain why you trust that absence of contradiction. A second stakeholder who fully confirms your framing is worth noting but also worth scrutinizing.

## Evaluation

Your submission will be evaluated on three things:

- Whether your interview plan shows deliberate question design connected to your problem frame - not generic curiosity but questions aimed at the specific uncertainties in your current thinking.
- Whether your second stakeholder is genuinely different from your Sprint 1 contact in role or perspective, not just a different person with the same vantage point.
- Whether your notes capture what the stakeholder actually said, including anything that pushes back against the frame you brought into the room.

The quality of the contradiction matters more than the quality of the confirmation. If this conversation changed nothing, your submission should explain why you believe that is the right outcome.
