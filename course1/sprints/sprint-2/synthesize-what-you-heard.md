# Synthesize What You Heard

You have now talked to two people who see your problem from different angles. This assignment asks you to put those two perspectives in the same room and figure out what they tell you together.

This is not a summary of each interview. It is an analysis of what happens when you hold both views at once.

## What to submit

Write four sections in the text entry field below.

**1. Where they agree**

Identify two to four things both stakeholders said, described, or implied - even if they used different words. Be specific. "Both agreed the process takes too long" is less useful than "Both described a handoff between teams X and Y as the point where delays accumulate." Name the actual overlap, not the category it falls into.

**2. Where they disagree**

Identify two to four points where the perspectives conflict. This could be a factual disagreement about what is happening, a difference in who they think is responsible, a difference in what they think matters most, or a different read on what has already been tried.

Write each disagreement as a plain statement of the gap: "Stakeholder A said X. Stakeholder B said Y." Let the gap speak for itself before you interpret it.

**3. What each person sees that the other does not**

This is different from disagreement. It is about visibility. Your Sprint 1 contact may see things from where they sit that your Sprint 2 contact simply cannot see, and vice versa.

For each stakeholder, write one or two sentences describing something they said that the other person probably would not have said - not because they disagree, but because their role or position gives them a different line of sight.

**4. Which tensions are real**

Look at the disagreements and visibility gaps you named above. Some of them will matter for how you frame and scope your problem. Others will turn out to be differences in language or emphasis that dissolve when you look closely.

For at least one tension, explain:

- What the tension actually is
- Why it cannot be explained away as a language difference
- What it means for your problem frame or scope statement

If addressing this tension requires you to revise something in your scope statement, say so. If it surfaces a stakeholder you have not talked to yet, name them.

## Using AI

AI can be useful for organizing themes across two sets of notes - identifying patterns, clustering related observations, surfacing potential contradictions. If you use it, paste both sets of interview notes into the tool and ask it to identify where the accounts converge and diverge.

What AI cannot do: tell you which tensions matter. That judgment depends on your knowledge of your organization, your role, and what you are actually trying to change. Your synthesis should show that judgment clearly. If a reader cannot tell the difference between your interpretation and an AI-generated theme list, revise it.

A useful test: read your "Which tensions are real" section and ask whether it could have been written by someone who does not work at your organization. If the answer is yes, add the organizational specificity that only you can provide.

## A note on conflicting accounts

If your two stakeholders told you substantially different stories about the same events or processes, do not smooth that over. The conflict is data. It may mean the problem is more politically complex than your current frame captures. It may mean the consequences of the problem land differently depending on where you sit. It may mean there is a third perspective you need before your frame is solid.

Name what you found. Your job here is accurate description, not diplomatic resolution.

## Evaluation

Your submission will be evaluated on three things:

- Whether the agreements and disagreements you identify are concrete - specific enough that someone else could verify them against your interview notes.
- Whether you have identified at least one tension that is genuinely structural and have explained what it means for your problem frame.
- Whether your own judgment is visible in the synthesis - particularly in the section on which tensions are real. This section should read like someone who knows the organization, not like a neutral theme-extraction exercise.
