# Problem Spine

## What this document is

The layer above the sprints. It states what the course is for, how a participant arrives at a problem, what makes a problem workable, and what happens to that problem across the ten weeks.

Sprint artifacts should be built against this rather than against each other.

This version is a rebuild. The 19 August draft was lost, and four parts of it had gone stale during the Sprint 1 work anyway. Where this differs from that draft, Section 10 says so.

---

## 1. What the course is, and what it is not

The course takes one real problem and turns it into a problem clear enough to act on, with evidence that it is the right problem. It does not solve it. Sprint 5 produces a readiness judgment and an integrated problem document. Course 2, CIS 502, is where the solution gets built.

This has to be said plainly in the welcome, and it is: Welcome V2 already carries it.

**The claim the course makes.** AI makes answers cheap, which changes which problems are worth attempting. Things people gave up on, or never started, because working through them alone would have taken more time than they had, are now reachable. What has not changed, and has become more valuable, is knowing which problem is the right one.

Welcome V2 names the two ideas the whole course rests on, and sprint material should use its terms rather than inventing parallel ones:

- **Superagency.** Taking on work you would not otherwise have taken on. Tracked by evidence, not by feeling.
- **Human Value.** What you contribute that AI does not: context, relationships, judgment.

Under both sits **Symbiotic Thinking**.

## 2. How participants arrive at a problem

### There is no single route

An early draft of this spine treated noticing as the way in. That was too narrow. The real insight was smaller: *some* people have not labeled their frictions as problems. Others arrive with something obvious, or with something a manager handed them.

Almost nothing arrives already shaped like a problem. What arrives is an **indicator**: something that points at a problem without being one.

| The indicator | The move underneath |
|---|---|
| A situation you stopped noticing, because you built a work-around | What would this look like if you had never adapted to it? |
| A condition you think you cannot change | What specific thing inside this is hardest right now? |
| A decision you are avoiding | What is it that you cannot tell? |
| A complaint or frustration | Which version of this can you actually move? |
| A vague wish | What specifically went wrong the last time? |
| A solution somebody else already picked | Keep asking why until you reach the goal |

**Five of those six moves run the same direction:** broad, vague, or invisible, down to specific. The sixth runs the other way. A request is already specific; the trouble is that it is too settled. So the move goes up to the goal first, then back down to the problem. That asymmetry is why requests get the most instructional space.

### Work-arounds are the hardest to see

Work-arounds are rarely chosen. They accumulate. You route around something gradually enough that the routing becomes the normal way you do it, and if someone asked whether anything was wrong, you would say no. The problem stops being stored as a problem and gets stored as a habit.

This is not a flaw. Getting through hard things without stopping to examine every friction is a skill, and it is why people function. It is also why some things worth fixing stay invisible for years.

### Two kinds of arrival

Some participants arrive with a problem already in hand. The gathering step still applies to them, run as a check rather than a search: does the problem you brought hold up, and did anything else surface that you had not considered?

Material must account for both arrivals. A discovery exercise presented as the only path reads as busywork to someone who already knows what they are bringing.

### Where the problem may come from

Work, a job search, a role transition, community or volunteer work, caregiving, campus work, a small business, or another real setting where actual people are affected. Stated once, in the welcome, using the Welcome V2 language. Sprint headers should not each carry a different version of the list.

## 3. What makes a problem workable

**A problem is a gap between how something works now and how it could work, where the gap costs someone something real.** That is the whole bar, and it is wider than what usually gets called a problem at work.

Four checks, in order. Each is cleared by honest looking: enough attention to say the answer is plausibly not a definite no. Not verification. Sprint 3 is where verification happens.

1. **Real people.** Who specifically is affected, and what does it cost them? Named roles in a named setting, not a category: "parents in Phoenix after a haboob," not "parents."
2. **Currently handled, and falling short.** "X handles this, but it does not ___."
3. **Live now.** Is this happening currently, or is it a memory or a hypothetical?
4. **The right size.** Recast 4 September 2026; see the note below. Three tests, all required: you can describe how it works now in one paragraph; there is at least one person other than you with a stake in it or knowledge about it, who would talk to you about it; there is at least one thing you would have to find out to move on it.

> **Note, 4 September 2026.** Check 4 previously read "Testable at this size: could you find out whether you are right within ten weeks?" That wording was vague and caught neither way a problem actually dies later. Wrong size fails in two opposite directions. **Too small:** only you touch it, one decision resolves it, nothing left to find out. **Too big:** you cannot describe the current state, nobody owns it, everyone is affected so you can name no one. The fix for the first is to go up and ask what this is an instance of; for the second, to go down and pick one instance. So check 4 is a calibration rather than a gate, and its three tests map onto what half two, Sprint 3, and Sprint 4 each demand, which means a failure names the week the problem would have stalled in. Taught in Sprint 1 half one, section E5.

> **Note, 9 September 2026.** Three rulings from the Sprint 1 build:
> 1. **Week one screens for frameability, not solvability.** Whether the problem can be solved in the partner course is Sprint 5's readiness judgment, the existing gate between the courses. Check 2 therefore stays local in Sprint 1 ("who copes with this here"); the world-scan of what already exists is Sprint 2's widening, and the Sprint 2 rebuild must carry it.
> 2. **The cost of a problem can fall entirely on the learner.** What every problem needs is one reachable person outside the learner who is affected or holds knowledge, since Sprint 3 needs information, not a victim. Check 4's second test now says so.
> 3. **Caring about the problem is not a fifth check.** Checks rule candidates out; caring ranks survivors. It lives at commitment: a required why-you line in the reasons, and a bored-sole-survivor exit back to the list.

Failing a check rules the problem out, and that is the point. A check that never rules anything out is not doing anything. Expect one workable problem to emerge from every three to five considered.

### The question underneath the four

**Are you stuck on understanding, or stuck on execution?** If you know exactly what to do and have not done it, that is capacity or discipline, and neither AI nor this course helps much. If you cannot act because you do not understand the situation well enough, that is a framing problem.

This is a better criterion than "would AI help with this," which pushes participants toward problems whose output is a document and away from problems that are about people changing behaviour.

## 4. What does not work

Four things look like problems and are not: **conditions**, **decisions**, **grievances**, and **wishes**. Each has a real problem inside it; none can be worked as stated. They appear in Section 2's table with the move that gets underneath each.

**Invented cases** do not work either, because you will not stay with one for ten weeks.

### The household test for personal problems

Caregiving and family problems are eligible, and some are excellent. The test: **can you name at least one person outside your household you could talk to about this, and would it be normal to ask them?**

A caregiving logistics problem passes, because coordinators, pharmacists, and other caregivers exist and are normal to ask. A problem that lives entirely between you and your own teenager does not, because interviewing your child with prepared questions is not the conversation Sprint 3 asks for. (Reworded 9 September 2026: "stakeholder" removed, since that is Sprint 3 vocabulary and the person can be a knowledge-holder rather than someone affected.)

## 5. The arc

| Sprint | What happens to the problem | Where the thinking sits |
|---|---|---|
| Welcome | No problem needed yet. Say so explicitly. | - |
| 1 | Gather candidate problems, run the four checks, commit to one, frame it | Your own head |
| 2 | Test the frame against alternatives and against what already exists. Produce a confirmation plan naming what is unverified and who could verify it | Your head plus AI |
| 3 | The confirmation plan meets a real person. The frame survives, shifts, or breaks | Outside your head |
| 4 | The problem stops moving and starts making demands on you. Name the gap blocking your next move, close it, and check what you learned against something that is not AI | Back to you: capability |
| 5 | Judge readiness and hand the problem to an outside reader | Outward |

**The hinges are real and already built.** Sprint 2's confirmation plan is what Sprint 3 executes. Sprint 3's conversation is what opens the gap Sprint 4 closes; Sprint 4 V3's opening says so directly. Sprint 4's learning evidence becomes a section of Sprint 5's integrated document.

**Sprint 1's hand-off is the riskiest assumption**, named in section 7 of the Goal Plan, which seeds Sprint 2's confirmation plan. If Sprint 2 is rebuilt, it must be built to receive that.

## 6. How AI is used

### Five capacities, one per sprint

AI appears in every sprint in a different capacity. None of them is absence.

| Sprint | Capacity |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Widen.** Open up a frame you are too close to see around |
| 2 | **Direct.** Supply context, constraints, and standards, then judge what comes back |
| 3 | **Rehearse.** Prepare questions and anticipate what you will hear, then find out where you were wrong |
| 4 | **Learn from.** Build a learning path without outsourcing the learning, and check it against something that is not AI |
| 5 | **Attribute.** Name what you contributed that AI could not |

Sprint 3's capacity is currently stated only as a prohibition in its V2 draft: review the Dojo output as preparation, not evidence, and do not treat the Dojo as a stakeholder. Rehearsal is a real use of AI and should be stated positively.

### The course-level commitment

Separate from the per-sprint capacities: **you own the goal, the standards, the choices, and the final meaning of the work.** This belongs at course level, not inside any sprint's AI learning goal, and it already appears in every sprint's working standard.

### Why think first: the mechanism

The habit is stated in at least four places and enforced in one graded artifact. The gap is that it is delivered as an instruction rather than a reason.

The reason: **it is hard to judge what AI hands you on a problem you have not thought through yourself.** Fluent text is hard to evaluate without an independent view to check it against. That is not a failure of skepticism. Judgment needs material, and the material is your own prior view.

Many participants will not be used to arriving at a situation having done their own thinking, or to forming a position while new information is still arriving. Both are practised skills. The course should say so and require practice rather than assume it.

### Real pushback versus performed pushback

Asking for critical engagement produces people who write "I pushed back on the AI's suggestion" because a rubric asked for it. The observable difference is that real pushback is **specific and costly**: it names what was rejected and what was lost by rejecting it.

> Performed: The AI's suggestions were too generic, so I made them more specific.

> Real: It suggested framing this as a communication problem. That is probably partly right. I dropped it because it points toward training, and I think the real issue is that nobody owns the handoff. I might be wrong about that.

Checkable by looking, which is the bar for a rubric row.

### One asset already built

The Dojo's own core instructions enforce think-first at the level of every turn. It is told to ask for the participant's read before offering its own, and to take one step rather than completing a partial answer. One line in it is better than anything currently in the course text: *handing someone the conclusion and asking them to own it is not the same as making them reach it.*

The tool already embodies the pedagogy. The course text mostly does not. Closing that gap is largely a matter of making activities match what the Dojo already does.

| Sprint | What you write before AI | Currently exists? |

## 8. Exit rules

The course signals in five places that the problem is carried through all five sprints, and never says what happens if it does not survive.

| Window | Status |
|---|---|
| Sprint 1 | Candidate problems are meant to be ruled out. This is the exercise |
| Sprint 2 | Open. Switching is free |
| Sprint 3 | Last real exit. If the conversation shows the problem is already handled, is not real, or is not current, that is a finding and the deliverable needs somewhere to record it |
| Sprints 4 and 5 | Closed. You revise the frame, not the problem |

**Changed from the earlier draft:** these windows are no longer stated up front in Sprint 1. Telling someone in week 1 that switching is free through Sprint 2 is procedural detail about a future they cannot picture, and it implies picking wrong is a live danger with a deadline. Sprint 1 says instead that you will try on more than one. Sprints 2 and 3 should carry the message where it becomes actionable.

Sprint 5 already permits "not ready yet, with a clear reason and next step" as a readiness judgment. That is the course's only sanctioned negative outcome, and earlier exits are consistent with it.

The distinction the course must draw: **revising the frame** is expected throughout. **Changing the problem** is a different act with a closing window.

|---|---|---|
| 1. Real people | Sprint 1 | Sprint 3 |
| 2. Currently handled, falling short | Sprint 1, honest looking | Sprint 2, widened with AI |
| 3. Live now | Sprint 1 | Sprint 3, by whether anyone will talk about it |
| 4. The right size | Sprint 1 | Split: half two by whether it can be framed, Sprint 3 by whether anyone will talk, Sprint 4 by what it demands you learn |

Check 2 is the one with the weakest home downstream. Sprint 1 now teaches it as one of four checks, at honest-looking depth. Sprint 2 is where it should be widened, and Sprint 2 currently asks AI for choices and tradeoffs about solution directions, never about what already exists.

## 11. Three rules the Sprint 1 work produced

These came out of building, not planning. They belong here because they apply to every sprint.

**The register rule.** Lead with what is being released, not with what is at stake. Put the exit before the commitment. Sprint 1's first drafts opened with "everything you do for the next ten weeks runs on the problem you choose here," which was both heavy and untrue, since candidate problems are meant to be ruled out.

**The vocabulary rule.** Use the terms the course has already established rather than arguing the same ideas in new words. Welcome V2 defines Superagency, Human Value, and Symbiotic Thinking. A participant should not meet two framings of one idea two pages apart.

**The term-slot rule.** A term gets a numbered slot in a sprint's Concepts section if the reader's everyday meaning would mislead them, or if the sprint runs on it. Otherwise it is defined where it is first used. This is sprint-scoped: it predicts Sprint 3 numbering *stakeholder* and *validation conversation*, which its Concepts section already does. The second half of the rule is the one that will creep, since "important" can be argued for anything.

A fourth, smaller one: **the Introduction is orientation, not instruction.** If a header inside a Concepts section feels like it should be a subheader of another header in that section, it is a layer too deep for the Introduction and belongs on a concept page or in the assignment where it is used.

## 12. Unresolved elsewhere in the course

Sprint 1 is not the only place with open work. Recorded here so it does not get lost when attention moves.

### Sprint 3 V2: specified but not built

Four items in Melisa's spec did not reach the build:

- The worked validation-conversation example, the onboarding case with a weak Question A and a strong Question B.
- The requirement to identify where confirmed information came from. The build has Confirmed and Inferred but no field for the source, so "confirmed" is a stronger assertion with nothing behind it.
- The lead-in sentence before the stakeholder relationship bullets.
- The Dojo how-to video. The sentence is there, ending in a colon, with no link.

Also a plain bug: the Stakeholder Conversation says it has three parts and then lists four.

### Sprint 3 V2: tensions with this spine

- **Stakeholder categories assume a workplace.** Affected, influential, and authority-or-constraint is org-chart language. This spine admits caregiving, job search, and community problems, which need a stated path through the same exercise.
- **No sprint-level AI capacity.** Section 6 names it as rehearsal; V2 states it only as a prohibition.
- **The frame is revisable but the problem is not killable.** Section 8 makes Sprint 3 the last exit, which means the deliverable needs somewhere to record a problem that did not survive the conversation.
- **Confirmed and Inferred marking** should be continuous with Sprint 1's softer ancestor rather than arriving unannounced.
- **Worked weak-and-strong contrasts** are used well in V2 and should be a course-wide pattern rather than a Sprint 3 device.

Five reviewer threads on that document remain open: the fourth stakeholder category, whether the concept check should be an activity rather than a quiz, what the Dojo is actually required for, whether "map" is the right word, and whether participants may use their own AI tools.

### Peer interaction: the audit

- **The course has no participant-to-participant interaction.** Every activity named Peer Exchange carries `delivery_mode: ai_activity`. The word "peer" is attached throughout to activities involving no peers, which is worse than an absence because it hides the gap.
- **The only real human contact** is Sprint 3's stakeholder conversation, and a stakeholder is not a peer. Welcome V2's Introduction Post is the single exception.
- **Usable instructions already exist in metadata and are invisible.** Every sprint has a `verify` line in `homepage.yaml`. Sprint 1: ask another person to read the problem statement and name what they think you are actually trying to solve. Sprint 2: show one AI output and your pushback notes to a peer. No participant will ever see these.
- **The other person does not have to be a classmate.** Explaining yourself to someone outside the course is free, fully asynchronous, works at any pace, and needs no tooling or cohort. Available now regardless of the pacing decision, and Sprint 1 V2 uses it in three places.
- **The Commons** has been running peer conversations since Spring 2026. Its campfire mechanic, post before you read, is structurally the same discipline as Section 7, pointed at peers rather than at AI. Whether course1 can use it is unknown, since course1 is Canvas-native.
- **The blocker is pacing, not tooling.** Two participants six weeks apart cannot exchange anything. This decision precedes any tool question.

1. **Root Cause Analysis naming.** The brief calls the Sprint 1 output a Root Cause Analysis. The existing artifact is the Goal Plan and Problem Reframing Document. Sprint 1 V2 calls it the Goal Plan and Problem Frame. Three names, and Sprint 0's glossary is blocked on it.

4. **Cohort or self-paced.** This governs whether any peer mechanic is possible. Already recorded in `context/open-questions.md`.
5. **Peer interaction.** See Section 12 for the audit. The one piece that does not depend on the pacing decision: surface the `verify` lines into artifacts, framed as explaining your work to someone outside the course.
6. **Whether the course is one arc or five sprints.** Sprints 3, 4, and 5 hand off cleanly to each other. Sprint 1 to Sprint 2 is the join that has never been built deliberately, because Sprint 2 has not been rebuilt.
7. **The artifact titles Find Your Candidates and Test Your Candidates** use "candidate" alone, while body text always says "candidate problem."
