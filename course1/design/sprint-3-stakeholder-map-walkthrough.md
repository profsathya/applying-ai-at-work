### Stakeholder Map Canvas Walkthrough, 50 points

Submission: one Word file with four stakeholder tables, two or three assumption blocks, and the first-contact table. Lines 2 to 5 are judged across all four stakeholder tables. Lines 6 and 7 are judged across the assumption blocks. The outreach log is optional and is not scored.

Total: 50. Passing: 35. Partial on every line gives 36.

Gate: fewer than two stakeholder tables is not a pass, and a map with no assumption block is not a pass, whatever the other lines score.

Grading note. Lines 2 to 4 score once across the four stakeholder tables: full when all four pass, 3 when three do, 1 when two or fewer do. Line 5 scores once across the 24 fields to mark (six in each table; the question field is left out): full when all are marked with a reason, 3 when up to six are missing a mark or a reason, 1 when more than six are. Lines 6 and 7 score once across the assumption blocks. Read for meaning: if the idea is there in the learner's own words, give the points. A blank part is a 1 on its line, not a 0 on the item. A group, or a role without a name yet, counts as one stakeholder. On line 5, the "Next question to ask" field does not need a mark: a mark there, "Not applicable," or a blank all pass. "Partly confirmed" passes when it says which part is confirmed and which is a guess. On line 7, a question about what would make the person do or skip something passes, as long as it does not ask them to approve the idea; the page lists a similar question as strong. A blank Assumption 3 is not a miss. Every rung below full names the table and the field it rests on, or the assumption block and the line. On line 5, name the tables that have the gaps, not each field, and ask the learner to review the Confirmed or Inferred column there. The ranks put the count and the relationship first, then the assumption blocks: every later step in the sprint is built on the four stakeholder tables, so a gap there is the first thing the learner should hear (Melisa, 1 October).

What makes it strong. The fields marked Inferred are the ones the assumption blocks go after. The question in each block grows out of a "Next question to ask" in a stakeholder table. The first choice is the person who can answer the top assumption.

Open.
1. Eight lines. The guide's target is five. Lines 3 and 4 could merge, and so could 6 and 7, if the first batch shows they fail together.
2. Lines 2 to 4 use the stricter standard: three of four tables for the 3 rung (Melisa, 1 October). Check against the first batch whether this is too strict.
3. The page's final check says "Your fields show real thinking." A grader cannot see that. Lines 3 and 4 are the observable version; the page line should change to match.
4. The page tells learners to mark the question field Confirmed or Inferred ("For the question field, mark whether the concern behind your question..."). Line 5 no longer asks for that, and the worked example writes "Not applicable." Remove that sentence from the page, and change the final check line "All seven fields are complete for every stakeholder, and each one is marked..." to match.
5. Line 1 has a small space between its rungs: three stakeholders with one or two fields blank. Decide where that lands when the first batch comes in.
6. All rungs are a best guess until the first batch is graded.

**Grader note.** Read for meaning. If the idea is there in the student's own words, give the points. A blank row earns 0. A Problem Frame that held, with a reason, earns full points; no change is a valid result.

**What makes it strong.** The assumption in Section A is the one the questions go after. What was confirmed or challenged in Section C shows up in Section D and in the updated Problem Frame.

**Grading, in the open.** Do most of each part and you pass. Do all of it for full points. A partial comment doubles as the nudge: which section, which row, what is missing.

**Weighting by effort.** Section A 10%, questions 18%, Section B 20%, Section C 14%, Section D 18%, updated Problem Frame 20%. Section B is weighted for the conversation itself, not only the writing.

### Suggested updates to the student "before you submit" box

Two current bullets ask for things a grader cannot see on the page.

- "Your three validation questions are sharpened, not just copied." Suggested: "Each of your three questions asks about an experience, a decision, or a tradeoff."
- "Section C records your first impressions right after the conversation." Suggested: "Section C answers all four rows."

The box does not mention the updated Problem Frame. Suggested addition to the last bullet: "Your Problem Frame, all seven parts, is brought up to date."

### Principles used

1. **Two layers.** The short "before you submit" box is the student's quick check. The rubric is the full grading breakdown behind it.
2. **Built on Sathya's Self-check rule and Leslie's Sprint 1 example.** Neutral, observable lines; points per row; full, partial, and minimal levels.
3. **Grade only what is on the page.** No row depends on what the student knows or could do outside the assignment.
4. **Read for meaning, not wording.** Short or plain answers earn the points if the idea is there.
5. **Points follow effort.** Rows are weighted by how much students actually write: about 66% stakeholder tables, 24% assumption blocks, 10% first contact.
6. **Each thing is graded once.** No blank field loses points in two rows.
7. **Clear on full, generous on partial.** The full-points line states exact counts. Partial covers "most of it." Minimal is for real gaps.
8. **Every cell is a full statement.** Numbers are written out, and no cell relies on another to make sense.
9. **Partial everywhere equals a pass.** Partial is set near 70% of each row, so a student who does most of each part reaches 35 of 50.
