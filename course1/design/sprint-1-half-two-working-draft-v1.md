## The problem frame

*Page. Position 8.*

You have committed to one problem. The first half of this sprint asked you to look widely and hold loosely. The second half asks the opposite: go deep on the one you chose, and take it seriously.

Going deep means writing a **problem frame**: your account of the problem, specific enough that someone who knows the situation could disagree with it, with what you do not yet know marked as clearly as what you do.

A full frame has seven parts:

1. **The goal it serves.** What should be different, stated so nobody involved would dispute it.
2. **The problem.** The gap standing between now and that goal, specific enough to argue with.
3. **Who is affected, and what it costs them.** Named people or roles, and the cost in time, money, errors, or strain.
4. **How it is handled today, and where that falls short.** The system, process, habit, or person coping with it now, and the exact place it stops working.
5. **What fixed would look like.** A changed state, not a solution. "New hires have working access on day one" is a changed state. "Build an onboarding app" is a solution.
6. **What you are assuming.** Each claim in parts 1 to 5 that you have not confirmed, marked **unverified**.
7. **What you would need to find out next, and who could tell you.** The first two or three questions, and a person or role for each.

> **Illustrative worked example · Report formatting**
>
> **Goal:** Analysts spend their time on analysis, not on reformatting the same export every month.
>
> **Problem:** The export's column order does not match the report, and every analyst fixes it separately.
>
> **Affected, and the cost:** Fifteen analysts, about thirty minutes each per month. (?) Nobody has counted it.
>
> **Handled today:** Each analyst reorders columns by hand. It does not reach anyone who could change the export.
>
> **Fixed looks like:** One corrected copy, or an export that matches the report.
>
> **Assuming:** That all fifteen do the same fix. (unverified) That nobody has asked for the export to change. (unverified)
>
> **Find out next:** Whether the export can be changed, and who owns it. Ask the reporting team lead.

Almost nobody fills this in from the top down. You will start with the parts your table already holds, parts 2, 3, and 4, and work outward. Part 1 usually comes last, even though it is listed first.

A frame never contains a solution. If part 5 names something to build, you have not gone far enough.

Continue to **Your first frame**.

## Your first frame

Write version one of your frame from what you already have. Rough is right. A part you cannot fill gets the words "I do not know yet," and that line is part of the deliverable, not a failure of it.

Work without AI on this one. Version one is your own read of the problem. The next activity uses AI to widen it, and widening only works if there is an independent view to widen from.

### 1. Find the goal underneath

Take your committed problem from **Test and commit** and ask **why**, then **why does that matter**, until you reach something nobody involved would dispute and that says nothing about what to build. That is part 1 of your frame. You did this move on requests in **Things somebody handed you**; now run it on your own problem.

### 2. Write out your assumptions

Go back to your four-line candidate and your check answers. Every **(?)** is an assumption. Write each one as a plain sentence: "I am assuming that ___." Three to six is normal. These become part 6 of your frame.

### 3. Fill the seven parts

Write your frame using the seven labels from the previous page. Parts 2, 3, and 4 come from your table and your candidate. Part 5 is a changed state, not a fix. Part 7 is your best guess at the first two questions and who could answer them. Mark anything unverified.

Keep this version exactly as you wrote it. The reflection at the end of the sprint compares it with your final frame, and that only works if version one stays unedited.

## Dojo Lab: widen the frame

*Own your progress. Position 10. 0 points. Submit to mark it complete.*

Bring your first frame. This activity uses AI in the one way Sprint 1 asks for: to open up a frame you are too close to see around. AI can generate ten framings of your problem in four seconds. It cannot know which one your director would fund or which colleague would quietly block it. You can. That is the division of labor here.

Use the Symbiotic Thinking Dojo if you have it set up, or any AI chat. Do not paste confidential workplace, client, or personal details. Change names and drop anything sensitive before you start.

> **[NOTE]** Tool wording depends on the Dojo and API key decision. The prompts below run unchanged in any chatbot.

### 1. Ask for three other ways to see it

Paste your frame and use this prompt:

> Here is my problem frame. Give me three other ways to see the same situation. For each one: what it makes visible that my frame does not, what it would make me miss, and who would have to be involved. Then tell me which of the three would be hardest to find out about in ten weeks, and whether any of them suggests this problem is already handled somewhere.

Read the three framings against your own situation. One will usually be generic and worth ignoring. One will usually land.

### 2. Ask where you are asserting rather than knowing

Follow up with:

> Now look at the claims in my frame. Which ones am I asserting rather than knowing? Where would someone who works in this area disagree with me? Do not rewrite the frame.

### 3. Decide what changes

Record two or three challenges from the exchange and your response to each. A real response names what you rejected and what you lose by rejecting it. "Fair, I do not actually know that" and "No, I have seen this directly, here is how" are both good responses. Agreeing with everything is not, and neither is dismissing all of it.

Then decide: which framing, if any, changes your frame; which claims move from assumed to marked for verification; and what stays as it was.

### Response tasks

- **framings** (response). Prompt: *The three framings, one line each, and which one, if any, you are keeping.* Criteria: three framings summarized in the participant's words; a decision stated for each; at least one is rejected with a reason.
- **challenges** (response). Prompt: *Two or three challenges from the exchange and your response to each.* Criteria: each response names what was rejected or accepted and why; at least one response is a specific "no, here is how" or a specific "fair, I do not know that"; no response is a bare agreement.

## Goal Plan and Problem Frame

*Graded item. Position 11. 50 points. One submission.*

> **[NOTE]** Name still open: Goal Plan and Problem Frame, Root Cause Analysis, or another. This draft keeps the name the Introduction's road map already uses.

This is the document the rest of the course builds on. It has four parts and one submission. Your instructor reads for a frame that is specific, honestly marked, and moving: what changed since version one and why.

### Part 1. Frame version two

Rewrite your frame with what the Dojo Lab changed. All seven parts. Mark each assumption in part 6 as **confirmed** or **unverified**. Confirmed means you have seen it yourself or someone who knows told you. Everything else is unverified, and that is fine at this point.

### Part 2. What changed, and why

One short paragraph. Name what moved between version one and version two, and what evidence or argument moved it. Include one thing the Dojo Lab suggested that you rejected, and why. A frame that did not change is a legitimate result if you can say what you tested it against.

### Part 3. Your riskiest assumption

Pick the one assumption in your frame that, if it turned out to be wrong, would break the frame rather than adjust it. Write three lines: the assumption; what would have to be true for it to hold; and how you could find out. This line is what Sprint 2 works on first.

### Part 4. Who could tell you

Name two or three people or roles who could confirm or complicate your frame, and for each: what they know that you do not, and why you could reach them. They do not have to be affected by the problem. Someone who knows how it works from the inside counts. If your problem is personal, name someone outside your household whom it would be normal to ask.

> **Illustrative worked example · Report formatting, parts 3 and 4**
>
> **Riskiest assumption:** That all fifteen analysts do the same fix. If most have already automated it, the cost is a fraction of what I think and the problem is too small. To hold, at least ten would have to be doing it by hand. I could find out by asking three of them this week.
>
> **Who could tell you:** The reporting team lead, who knows whether the export can change. Two analysts on other teams, who know whether they do the same fix. Both are reachable; I sit near one.

In Sprint 2 you test this frame against alternatives and against what already exists elsewhere. If it does not survive that, Sprint 2 says what to do. Your runner-up from **Test and commit** is still in your table.

### Response tasks

- **frame-v2** (response). Prompt: *Your frame, version two, seven parts, assumptions marked confirmed or unverified.* Criteria: all seven parts present; part 5 is a changed state; every assumption carries a mark; at least one unverified assumption remains, since a frame with none at this stage is a warning sign.
- **what-changed** (response). Prompt: *What changed from version one, what moved it, and one suggestion you rejected.* Criteria: names a specific change or a specific reason for no change; names the evidence or argument; includes one rejected suggestion with a reason.
- **riskiest** (response). Prompt: *Your riskiest assumption, what would have to be true, and how you could find out.* Criteria: one assumption, not a list; a clear condition for it to hold; a way to find out that names a person or an observation.
- **who-could-tell-you** (response). Prompt: *Two or three people or roles, what each knows, and why you could reach them.* Criteria: named roles or people, not categories; each has a stated reason to be asked; at least one is reachable within two weeks.
