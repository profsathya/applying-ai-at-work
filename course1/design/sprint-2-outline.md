# Sprint 2: outline, v4

Short on purpose. Leslie is ruling on structure; the prose sketches that made v3 long are
gone, and the reasoning behind each choice is in the decisions log for 17 September.

## What Sprint 2 is for

Sprint 2 asks whether the problem chosen in Sprint 1 is worth the next eight weeks. The
activities help the participant consider that, and the judgment is theirs. By the end
they should be able to say, with reasons, that this problem seems worth pursuing, or
that it does not and which runner-up they are taking. A no is the sprint working, not
failing. The goal of the course is a workable problem, not completing the activities.

Three moves get them there:

1. **Which assumptions would break it.** An assumption is something written as if it
   were true. It is not a fact yet. Behind each one is a question, and the answer could
   change the problem or show that it is not worth working on. Walk the list, choose
   one to check this week, check it, and read the list back for what a change would ask
   of people.
2. **What already exists.** Brainstorm what could already solve or ease this, in your
   situation or out in the world; direct AI to widen and test the list; look at two or
   three for real. An existing solution does not kill a problem by existing. Getting it
   adopted where you are can be the problem worth solving.
3. **The verdict.** Worth pursuing, not worth pursuing, or depends on one named thing,
   written into the Problem Frame.

The AI capacity is **direct**, and the vocabulary is Sathya's three Cs as he uses them
across his courses: **Context** is what you provide; **Choices** are the decisions you make
about what comes back; **Confirmation** is checking the output against something real.
Sprint 2 teaches Context and practises the other two.

## The living artifact

**The Problem Frame is the artifact that carries through the course.** There is no
Confirmation Plan and no Goal Plan. Sprint 3 V3 already revises "your Problem Frame"; so
will Sprints 4 and 5 once their references are renamed.

In Sprint 2 the frame grows by a little, not a lot:

- **Part 6** gains one column, *Checked*, with what came back in one line, or "not yet."
  Most rows say "not yet" until a later sprint fills them.
- **Part 8, what a change would cost.** Four or five lines: what the problem costs now
  (from 3b), what any change would ask of whom, and the time to build anything.
- **Part 9, the verdict.** One paragraph.

Everything else Sprint 2 produces stays in the Log. At the end of the course the frame is
nine parts on two pages, the kind of document you could hand to a manager.

**The Log splits at the end of Sprint 1.** The Candidate Log stays a Sprint 1 document
(Parts A to D and the set-aside list). From the end of Sprint 1 the Problem Frame is its
own document, copied once from Part D. Each of Leslie's sprints gets a short worklog; the

## Each item, in a few lines

**2. Introduction.** Opens from the Problem Frame: you have one problem and a list of
things you assumed. Says what the sprint is for in Leslie's terms (above), and that a
verdict of no takes the runner-up's draft frame from First frames through a shortened
pass. Standard Sprint 1 sections: key terms, route by week, what to keep and submit, about
AI in this sprint, how to work, where to write. Names the two documents in play from here
on, the Problem Frame and the Sprint 2 Log.

**3. Which assumptions would break it.** The opening activity, Part 3 of Sprint 1's Problem
Frame item, which Jeremy's rebuild has already moved out of the published Sprint 1. It
opens with the why (an assumption is a question you have not asked yet). Then the walk,
loosely: for each assumption, what would you need to find out, and what could the answers
change; one question or several, uneven answers welcome. Then two judgments across the
whole list: which could end the problem, and what would a change ask of people. Then the
choice of one to check this week, with the guidance that the killing answer must be
plausible before it is worth checking, that some assumptions you can already make the call
on and should say so, and that reachable beats well-chosen. The check is written before
looking (the claim, what result would say it was wrong, who or what you will check it
against), in Sprint 4's form. It runs in the background of week 1 and is recorded in item
10. What comes back is a temperature check: information for a decision, not the decision.

The scaffolding is subordinate to the question. The activity opens with the problem, not
the deliverable; the Log slots are offered as a way to work; the worked example shows one
assumption the writer declines to check and says why; the criteria grade the reasoning;
and the activity ends, like every activity in the sprint, with the same question: what
does this change about how you would describe the problem?

**4. What could already solve this.** Before any AI. Three columns in the Log: what exists
(external or internal, formal or informal, whole or partial, anything even tangentially
related); how it works, as far as you know (the mechanism, to the standard Sprint 1's Get
underneath table set); where it might work for you, where it falls short, and what you do
not know. The third column is the list of things to find out.

**5. Directing AI.** The page Leslie asked for. Why you go first (fluent output needs a
view of your own to judge against; in this sprint that view is items 3 and 4). Then
Context in two halves: give AI the drafting you have done, which is easy, and give it what
the draft does not say, which is the skill. Two kinds: about the thing (your situation,
what is off the table, what you already know), and about how to work with you (what you
want back and in what shape, what it must not do, where you want to be asked rather than
told). The Dojo setup's "what is mine vs what you carry" is the same move. Choices and
Confirmation get a paragraph each and are practised in items 8 and 10. The undirected and
directed asks side by side, on the late-deliverable email from V1.

**6. Self-check: Context.** Replaces the concept check with something used ten minutes
later: a bare ask about the handover problem; write what you would add about the thing
and about how to work with you; name one thing you would check in whatever came back.

**7. Dojo Lab: what already exists.** Sprint 1's four-step Dojo flow, which Sprint 3 V3
also uses: bring what you wrote; test it; widen it; choose. The prompts laid out under
Context (what you paste and what you add), so the page shows the thing item 5 taught. AI
widens the list with items you did not think of, tests each for viability in your
situation and where it falls short, and names the two or three worth a real look. Then
the real look: open the product, read one account of someone who tried it, enough to say
whether it kills the problem, eases it, or is itself the thing your situation cannot
adopt. Runs in the participant's Dojo (the project set up in Welcome, with the course
document as its source) or in any chat. [NOTE] The prompt's wording waits on Leslie's own
test run; Claude runs it on the handover example first and hands over the transcript.

**8. What exists, and what it means.** Five boxes, one submission. What did you think
already existed, before you asked? What did AI add, and what did you make of it (kept, set
aside, not sure, with a reason; setting nothing aside is fine if you say why)? What did
you look at, and what did you learn? Does any of it change your problem (nothing fits;
something fits and adopting it is the problem; something fits and would simply solve it)?
What does this change about how you would describe the problem? Points concentrate in the
last two. No paragraph, no optional step.

**9. Dojo Lab: before you decide.** Bring the check's result, the assumptions walk, the
cost read-back, and what exists. Same four-step flow. AI tests what the check actually
settled and what it did not; which of the remaining assumptions matter most now; what the
cost read-back leaves out; and whether the pieces add up to a verdict or to one named
thing that still has to be found out. It does not write validation questions; Sprint 3 V3
owns those, with its assumption blocks and the weak-versus-strong teaching.

**10. Problem Frame: is it worth pursuing?** The terminal item. Bring the frame up to date
where Sprint 2 changed it: 4a and 4b if the research found something, part 6 with the
Checked column and any assumption confirmed, added, or dropped, part 8 the costs, part 9
the verdict with reasons. The check recorded in full (the claim, what would have made it
wrong, what you checked it against, what came back, what you make of it), or what you
tried and why it could not be run, which is an honest outcome graded as one. If the
verdict is no: which runner-up, its draft frame, and a shortened pass. A frame that did
not otherwise change is a legitimate result, said in one line.

**11. Reflection.** `ai_activity`, the Sprint 1 mechanism. Where did something outside your
head change your view, and where did AI change it; which did you push back on and what
did it cost you; the one thing you are least sure how to find out.
