# Sprint 2: outline, v5.2

Short on purpose. Leslie is ruling on structure. The reasoning behind each choice is in
the decisions log for 17 September.

## What Sprint 2 is for

Sprint 2 asks whether the problem chosen in Sprint 1 is worth the next eight weeks. The
activities help the participant consider that, and the judgment is theirs. By the end
they should be able to say, with reasons, that this problem seems worth pursuing, or
that it does not and which runner-up they are taking. A no is the sprint working, not
failing. The goal of the course is a workable problem, not completing the activities.

Two weeks, two questions, then a verdict:

1. **Could anything break it?** Look closely at the assumptions. An assumption is
   something written as if it were true, but it is something you do not really know,
   yet. Behind each assumption are questions, and the answers could change the problem
   or show that it is not worth working on. Walk your list, choose one to check this
   week, check it, and read the list back for what a change would ask of people.
2. **Explore what already exists.** Are there examples of people trying to solve this or
   similar problems, in your situation or out in the world? Start the table yourself,
   direct AI to widen and answer it, look at two or three for real. If you do find an
   existing solution, it does not necessarily mean your problem is not worth pursuing.
   It just might impact the way you solve your problem.
3. **The verdict.** Worth pursuing, not worth pursuing, or depends on one named thing,
   written in the Sprint 2 graded item. It is Sprint 2's answer, so it does not live in
   the Problem Frame.

The AI capacity is **direct**, in Sathya's vocabulary: **Context** is what you provide,
**Choices** are the decisions you make about what comes back, **Confirmation** is checking
the output against something real. What Sprint 2 teaches of this is Context in its first
sense, by doing it twice: think first, then hand AI your thinking. The other kinds of
context are mostly carried by the Dojo document for now. [NOTE] Directing AI as a thread
through the whole course is parked; Leslie will revisit in about a week.

**How AI is used, stated in the Introduction.** No AI on the items marked own work. The
Dojo Labs are where AI tests what you wrote. Same rule as Sprints 1 and 3.

## The living artifact

**The Problem Frame carries through the course.** No Confirmation Plan, no Goal Plan.
Seven parts for the whole course; every part is living. In Sprint 2 it grows by two
things only:

- **3c, what a change would ask of them.** Beside 3b, what the problem costs them. Not
  the cost of a particular solution; the cost of changing how this is handled at all:
  time, a habit someone has to change, what gets worse while it settles, who has to
  agree, and the time to build anything. Leslie's placement: the comparison sits inside
  one part instead of across the document.
- **Part 6** gains a *Checked* column, holding one of three things: "not yet"; what came
  back, in one line; or "accepted, because" with the reason. Accepting an assumption is
  a decision that the likely answer would not change what you do, or that you would push
  ahead anyway, said rather than left looking unexamined. The reason is the whole point.

Nobody would hand this document to a manager as it stands. It is the thinking you would
need to make the case, spoken or written, and the top of the document says so in
Leslie's words.

**Documents.** The Candidate Log stays a Sprint 1 document, with Part D in reading order
(v2.3) so it pastes into the Problem Frame document as it stands; the only reason to
reopen it later is the runner-up's draft frame. The Problem Frame is its own document
from the end of Sprint 1. Sprint 2 has one document besides it, a one-page What already
exists table. There is no Sprint 2 Log: everything else the sprint asks for runs on the
Canvas pages as guidance, example, and box, one step at a time, which is the course's
pattern and the one Leslie asked for. Nothing carries sprint to sprint except the
Problem Frame.

## Each item, in a few lines

**2. Introduction.** Opens from the Problem Frame: you have one problem and a list of
things you assumed. Says what the sprint is for in Leslie's terms, that a verdict of no
takes the runner-up's draft frame through a shortened pass, and how AI is used. Standard
Sprint 1 sections: key terms, the route by week, what to keep and submit, about AI in this
sprint, how to work, where to write (the Problem Frame document and the What already
exists table; everything else in the activity boxes).

**3. Which assumptions would break it.** Own work. Opens with the why (above). The walk,
loosely: for each assumption, what would you need to find out, and what could the
answers change; one question or several, uneven answers welcome. Two judgments across
the whole list: which could end the problem, and what would a change ask of people (plus
the one cost no assumption carries, the time to build anything). Then the choice of one
to check this week: the killing answer has to be plausible before it is worth checking;
some you can already make the call on, and should say so, which is what "accepted,
because" records; reachable beats well-chosen. The worked example accepts one assumption
with its reason (people resist any new step, and the writer would push through that, so
the answer does not change the plan) and checks another.
The check written before looking, in Sprint 4's form: the claim, what result would say
it was wrong, who or what you will check it against. Then the ending: the assumptions
not checked go into part 6 with what would need finding out, and Sprint 3's conversation
is built for them; checking one is the start. What comes back is a temperature check.
Every activity ends with the same question: what does this change about how you would
describe the problem?

The page runs as guidance, example, box, one step at a time, on Sathya's pattern: the why,
then the walk with an example at the top, then each judgment with its example and box.
The scaffolding stays subordinate to the question: the activity opens with the problem,
the worked example shows one assumption the writer declines to check and says why, the
criteria grade the reasoning. Parts are always named with their number (part 6, your
assumptions), never the number alone; a convention for the whole course.

**4. Dojo Lab: test your assumptions.** Short. Bring the walk and the choice. One capped
prompt in Sprint 1's test pattern: is the one you chose the right one to check; where
would someone who works in this situation push back; what would they have you rethink.
Fix the walk yourself. One closing line, the same in both labs: what context you gave
that helped, and what you would give earlier next time.

**5. Self-check.** Five points, completion. Anti-skim on the Introduction: the question of
the sprint, the no path, how AI is used, the three documents.

**6. Which assumptions, and your check.** The week 1 graded item, 20 points, and the
completion of the walk. Three boxes: the two or three assumptions that could end the problem, with why
these and not the others; the one being checked, written before looking, and why it was
chosen; what a change would ask of whom. Criteria grade the reasoning: says why, names a
person or an observation, does not hedge the cost.

**7. What could already solve this.** Own work, no AI, in the What already exists table.
Opens with three short paragraphs in place of the Directing AI page: what you did in the last two activities was Context in
Sathya's sense, and it is why the conversation was worth having; two other kinds of
context exist, and your Dojo carries most of that from the course document, so the one
line to add is what you want back and in what shape; Choices and Confirmation in a
sentence each, pointed at the third column and the real look. Then start the table in
the Log:

| What exists | How it works, as far as you know | What it means for your problem: pluses, minuses, questions |
|---|---|---|

Fill what you can. Things you know of, guesses, and questions all count, and blanks are
fine. Two branches of guidance: if you have ideas, a guardrail (you are listing what
exists, not designing; "an agenda tool" is a thing, "we should build one" is a solution
and waits); if you have nothing, prompts that turn the frame into questions to look into
(is there a product for this; is there a name for this kind of problem; how do people
who do this well do it; does anyone in your own organisation already handle it; what
happens in places with the same problem and more money, or less). The example shows a
table that is mostly questions, so nobody thinks that is a failed brainstorm.

**8. Dojo Lab: explore what already exists.** The goal is to find out what exists; AI is
there to help you think better about it. Sprint 1's four-step flow: bring the table;
AI answers the questions, adds rows, and says how each thing works; you write the third
column; then the real look at two or three, enough to say whether each kills the
problem, eases it, or is itself the thing your situation cannot adopt. Prompts laid out
by what they supply. Its response box collects the filled table. The five questions it
sets up, answered in item 9: what you had before you
asked; what AI added or answered, and what you made of it (kept, set aside, not sure,
with a reason; setting nothing aside is fine if you say why); what you looked at and
learned; does any of it change your problem (nothing fits; something fits and adopting
it is the problem; something fits and would simply solve it); what does this change
about how you would describe the problem. Same closing line as item 4. [NOTE] The
prompt's wording waits on a test run; Claude runs it on the handover example first and
hands over the transcript.

**9. What exists, and what it means.** Graded, 20 points. The five questions from the lab,
as the boxes of one submission. Points concentrate in the last two.

**10. Problem Frame: is it worth pursuing?** The terminal item, 45 points. The verdict
is written here, in its own box, and does not go into the frame. Before writing part 9, one
prompt on this page, in the Dojo: here is my frame, what the check said, what exists,
and what a change would ask; what does the check settle and not settle; does anything in
4a or 4b have to change; what did I leave out of the costs; can I say worth pursuing,
and if not, what is the one thing still missing. Then bring the frame up to date: 4a
and 4b if the research changed them; part 6 with the Checked column and any assumption
confirmed, added, or dropped; part 8; part 9 with reasons. The check recorded in full,
or what was tried and why it could not be run, which is an honest outcome graded as one.
If the verdict is no: which runner-up, its draft frame, and a shortened pass. A frame
that did not otherwise change is a legitimate result, said in one line.

**11. Reflection.** `ai_activity`, the Sprint 1 mechanism. Where did something outside
your head change your view, and where did AI change it; which did you push back on and
what did it cost you; the one thing you are least sure how to find out.
