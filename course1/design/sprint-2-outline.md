# Sprint 2: outline, v2

## What changed since 12 September

The 12 September outline was written against a Sprint 1 that no longer exists. Five things
moved, and each one changes what this sprint opens with, teaches, or hands on.

1. **What arrives is the Problem Frame, not "frame version 2 and the riskiest
   assumption."** Sprint 1 ends with one Problem Frame in seven parts. Part 6 is an
   assumptions list, each entry marked confirmed or unverified, with who could tell you.
   The Problem Frame item's Part 3 picks the two or three assumptions whose failure would
   break the frame. There is no single riskiest assumption anywhere, and no version
   numbers. The plan's ordering rule changes with it.
2. **The claim kinds land here.** Checkable, prediction, causal bet were cut from Sprint 1
   on 15 September because nothing there used them. Sprint 2 is where the checking happens,
   so it is where the kind earns its keep: the kind decides how an assumption gets checked.
   They get a page, a concept check, and a column in the terminal artifact.
3. **Sprint 1 already tests and widens the frame.** Its Dojo Lab has every draft frame
   tested by a skeptic and given three other framings. The 12 September "Dojo Lab: Test
   the Frame" would run that a second time on the same frame. Sprint 2's second Dojo Lab
   changes purpose: it plans the checks. Both labs are written as modules for the Dojo
   core, with an interim any-chatbot version.
4. **The proxy works.** The "AI proxy is down" risk is closed. The Reflection is
   `ai_activity` like Sprint 1's.
5. **Register and conventions are Sprint 1's.** Three to eight hundred words a page,
   guidance then example then box, two core examples carried over (account handovers and
   the garden plot), and the marker vocabulary. Named at the foot of this outline.

## What Sprint 2 is for

Sprint 2 receives one Problem Frame with its assumptions marked and does two things to it.
It widens Check 2, what already handles this problem and where that falls short, with AI
directed to look beyond the participant's own situation. And it produces a Confirmation
Plan: every assumption still unverified, sorted by kind, with who could check it and the
question or observation that would. Sprint 3 executes that plan with a real person.

The AI capacity is **direct**: supply context, constraints, and standards, then judge what
comes back. V1's Context, Choices, Confirmation vocabulary is still the one thing in V1
Sprint 2 that teaches rather than describes, and the outline keeps it.

Sprint 2 is where switching problems is free, and it says so here, where it is actionable.
If the widening shows the problem is already handled, not real, or not current, the
runner-up is in the Dojo Lab's your-choice box and the set-aside list is in the Candidate
Log. That is the finding the sprint was designed to produce, not a failure.

## Each item, briefly

**1. Module header.** Short text item, as in Sprint 1: the opening, the three things
practised this sprint (direct AI with context and standards, judge what comes back, plan
how a claim gets checked), and what you carry forward. One line pointing at the
Introduction for the route.

**2. Introduction: Direct AI deliberately.** Opens from the Problem Frame: you have one
problem and a list of things you assumed. This sprint finds out what already exists and
decides how each assumption gets checked. Keeps V1's same-task-two-ways example, the late
deliverable email, which is the best thing in V1. States the mechanism from the spine as a
reason, not a rule: fluent output is hard to judge without an independent view, so you
write yours first. Defines Context, Choices, Confirmation once. States the switching rule
and where the runner-up lives. Standard sections from Sprint 1: key terms, the route by
week, what to keep and submit, about AI in this sprint, how to work, where to write.

**3. Which assumptions first.** Exists only if Part 3 moves out of Sprint 1. Then it is
the opening activity: from part 6, pick the two or three assumptions whose failure would
break the frame rather than adjust it; for each, what would have to be true, and who
could tell you or what you could observe. The worked example is the handover Part 3
already written on the Sprint 1 page. If Part 3 stays in Sprint 1, this content is
received, not written, and the Confirmation Plan's ordering rule simply points at it.

**4. What handles this today.** Before any AI. Start from 4a and 4b of your frame, which
you already have: what copes with this now, and the moment it stops working. Add what is
new: your guess at how other people in your situation handle the same thing, and what you
would expect to find if you went looking. Name the mechanism, the person absorbing it,
and where it stops. Short, since the frame did most of the work. This is the independent
view the Dojo Lab's output is judged against, and it is the own-thinking-first move
Sprint 2 never had (spine, section 7). Handover example: the outgoing owner tells the
incoming owner; the guess is that other teams put ownership in the CRM or send a team
note, and that both fail when nobody is made to do them.

**5. Dojo Lab: what already exists.** The 3Cs made concrete. Context: the frame and the
"handles this today" note. Choices: three ways this problem is handled elsewhere, each
with what it depends on and what it costs. Confirmation: which of those you could check,
and how. Then the switching question, asked directly: does any of this suggest the
problem is already handled, not real, or not current. The output of this lab is the
material for item 8.

As a Dojo module, in the `dojo-module-problem-frame.md` format: what is mine vs what you
carry (the frame and the guess are mine; the standard of a good option, with dependency
and cost, is yours), rounds with named characters, start here. Draft rounds: 1, Auditor
reads the guess and names what it takes for granted; 2, Framer offers three ways this is
handled elsewhere, two lines each, with what each depends on; 3, Challenger asks for the
participant's read on each before saying which is generic; 4, Sensei asks the switching
question and gives the recap. Under an [INTERIM] marker, the any-chatbot prompt, capped:
three options of two lines each, one line on which the participant could check and how,
one line on whether the problem looks already handled.

**6. Three kinds of claim.** A reference page, on the model of The problem frame:
definition, the three kinds, the rule that decides the check, two worked examples. The
kinds and their checks:

- A **checkable** claim is about what is now, and someone could confirm it by looking or
  by telling you. Check it by asking that person, or by looking.
- A **prediction** is about what will happen. Nobody can confirm it now. Check it by
  gathering signal: has it happened before, what happens in situations like it.
- A **causal bet** says one thing causes another. It hides a checkable claim inside a bet.
  Split it: check the present-tense part now, and hold the link as the bet a later change
  would test.

Worked example A sorts the handover assumptions list: nothing reaches the rest of the team
(checkable, ask the other managers); managers act on stale ownership for days (checkable,
watch the next handover); one day is fast enough to matter (prediction, ask what fast
enough has meant before). Worked example B sorts the garden list, and its causal bet does
the teaching: they quit because they had no one to ask splits into two checkable claims
(they had no one to ask; they quit) and the link, which stays a bet. The page also answers
Leslie's 15 September question, why care which kind an item is, in one sentence: because
the kind tells you whether to ask, to look, or to wait.

**7. Concept check.** Five or six questions on the kinds page and the Introduction: which
kind is this claim; which check fits; a causal bet split correctly or not; the 3Cs
applied to the email example; the switching rule. Written after the pages are drafted and
verified against them; re-verify if the pages move.

**8. AI output assessment and pushback.** The graded item, because directing AI becomes
observable here. Take one output from Dojo Lab 1. Judge it against the standard you set
before you asked, which is your "handles this today" note. Write the pushback in the form
the spine requires: what you rejected, why, and what you lost by rejecting it. Then what
you kept, and what you marked to check, with its kind. Closing step, optional: show the
output and your pushback to someone outside the course and record what they said. This
is the verify line from the homepage metadata, surfaced.

Handover example of real pushback: it offered "make handovers a communication problem
and train for it," which is probably partly right; dropped because it points at
training, and the frame says nobody owns the handover; may be wrong about that. Three
boxes: the output and the standard; the pushback; kept, rejected, to check.

**9. Dojo Lab: plan the checks.** Replaces the 12 September "Test the Frame." Bring the
assumptions list, every unverified entry, and the kinds page. For each assumption, the
participant names the kind and the check that fits; AI challenges. Draft rounds: 1,
Auditor reads the list and names any assumption whose kind is wrong or whose check does
not fit its kind; 2, Challenger takes the causal bets one at a time and makes the
participant split each into the present-tense claim and the bet; 3, Challenger takes the
questions one at a time and asks whether each would get evidence or approval, and what
the person could say that would change the participant's mind; 4, Sensei asks which
check comes first and why, and gives the recap. What is mine vs what you carry: the
assumptions and every question are mine; the three kinds and their checks, and the
evidence-not-approval test, are yours. Under an [INTERIM] marker, the any-chatbot
prompt, capped: three findings, ranked, naming the assumption and the kind, one question
someone who works in the situation would ask.

**10. Confirmation Plan.** The terminal artifact, in three parts and one submission, on
the model of the Problem Frame item.

Part 1, the Problem Frame brought up to date. Only where Sprint 2 changed it: 4a and 4b
if the widening found something, part 6 with any assumption Sprint 2 confirmed or added.
A frame that did not change is a legitimate result, said in one line.

Part 2, the plan. One row per unverified assumption: the assumption, its kind, who could
confirm or complicate it, the question you would ask or the thing you would observe. The
break-the-frame assumptions from Part 3 come first. Causal bets appear as their split
halves. Then two or three people named as candidates for the Sprint 3 conversation, with
one line each on why, and which assumption each could settle.

Part 3, the switching decision. Either one line that the problem survived the widening,
or one paragraph on what the widening showed and which runner-up or set-aside item you
took, and its frame, brought to the same state.

Worked example: the handover plan, six rows sorted by kind, the two Part 3 assumptions on
top, the account lead and one other manager as candidates.

**11. Reflection.** `ai_activity`, JSON upload, the Sprint 1 mechanism. Prompt: where did
your judgment change what AI gave you, and where did AI change your frame or your list.
Both directions, with one example each, and the one assumption you are least sure how to
check. aiContext on the Sprint 1 pattern: catch a pushback with nothing lost by it, a
change attributed to AI that the participant made, and a check that is really a to-do.
