# Sprint 2: outline, v3

## What Sprint 2 is for

Sprint 2 asks one question of the problem chosen in Sprint 1: **is this worth pursuing, and
what would have to be true?** Three moves answer it.

1. **Which assumptions would break it.** From the Problem Frame's assumptions list, the
   two or three whose failure would make this not worth nine weeks. One of them gets
   checked inside the sprint, by asking one person one question or by going to look at
   one thing.
2. **What already exists.** Products, practices, and other people with the same problem,
   and what they did or failed to do. The participant brainstorms first, directs AI to
   widen and test the list, then looks at the two or three most viable. An existing
   solution does not kill a problem by existing. Getting it adopted in your situation can
   be the problem worth solving.
3. **What a change would cost.** Nothing in the course yet asks this. Solving is not all
   upside: your time, who has to do something differently, what breaks while it settles.
   Weighed against what the problem costs now (3b of the frame).

The terminal artifact is the **Confirmation Plan**: the frame brought up to date, the one
check and what came back, every unverified assumption sorted by kind with the check that
fits it, and a verdict: worth pursuing, not worth pursuing, or depends on one named thing.
The plan is the living document the later sprints revise. Sprint 3 executes its top rows
with a real person, Sprint 4 revises it after the learning, Sprint 5 checks it. It replaces
the Goal Plan, which V1 named and never defined.

The AI capacity is **direct**: supply context, constraints, and standards, then judge what
comes back. V1's Context, Choices, Confirmation vocabulary carries; it is the one thing in
V1 Sprint 2 that teaches rather than describes.

**The goal is a workable problem, not the checkbox work.** Sprint 2 says so in its module
header and Introduction, and the same line is owed to Sprint 1's, for Jeremy's build map
after his readback. A verdict of "not worth pursuing" is the sprint working, not failing.

## Each item, briefly

**1. Module header.** The question of the sprint in one line. The three things practised:
find the assumptions that would break your problem and check one; direct AI to find what
already exists and judge what it says; weigh what a change would cost. What you carry
forward: a Confirmation Plan and a verdict. The goal is a workable problem; a no is a
result.

**2. Introduction: Is this problem worth pursuing?** Opens from the Problem Frame: you have
one problem and a list of things you assumed. This sprint finds out whether it is worth the
next eight weeks, and what you would have to check. Keeps V1's same-task-two-ways example,
the late deliverable email, for the 3Cs. States the mechanism as a reason: fluent output
is hard to judge without an independent view, so in this sprint you write yours first,
twice. States the no path: the runner-up's draft frame is in your Log, and the shortened
pass it takes. Standard sections from Sprint 1: key terms (Confirmation Plan, the three
kinds of claim named and deferred to their page, viable), the route by week, what to keep
and submit, about AI in this sprint, how to work, where to write.

**3. Which assumptions would break it.** Part 3 of the Problem Frame item, moved here as
the opening activity. From part 6, pick the two or three assumptions whose failure would
make this not worth pursuing rather than adjust the frame. For each: what would have to be
true, and who could tell you or what you could observe. Then choose one to check now,
and write the check before you look: the claim stated so it could be false, what result
would tell you it was wrong, who or what you will check it against. The worked example is
the handover Part 3 already written on the Sprint 1 page, plus its check (the three other
managers, this week: if fewer than two describe the same thing, it is one person's
habit). The check runs in the background of week 1 and is reported in the Confirmation
Plan.

**4. What could already solve this.** Before any AI. Your own brainstorm of what already
exists that could solve or ease this problem: in your own situation (a tool nobody uses, a
practice another team has), and out in the world (products, methods, what people in your
position usually do). For each, one line on where you think it falls short for your
situation. Rough and honest; the point is to have your own list before AI hands you one.
This is the independent view the Dojo Lab's output is judged against, and the
own-thinking-first move Sprint 2 never had. Handover example: a shared ownership field
in the CRM, a handover checklist, a weekly team note; the guess is that all three exist
somewhere and fail when nobody is made to do them.

**5. Dojo Lab: what already exists.** The 3Cs made concrete. Context: the frame, the goal
(part 1), how it is handled today (4a and 4b), and your brainstorm. Choices: more items,
and for each item on the combined list, is it truly viable for your situation, and where
does it fall short and how. Confirmation: which two or three are worth a real look, and
what you would need to learn about each to tell whether it changes your problem. Then the
real look: open the product, read one account of someone who tried it, enough to say
whether it kills the problem, eases it, or is itself the thing your situation cannot
adopt. The output of this lab is the material for item 8.

As a Dojo module, in the `dojo-module-problem-frame.md` format. What is mine vs what you
carry: the frame, the goal, and the brainstorm are mine, and so is the judgment of what
my situation can adopt; the standard of a viable option (it addresses 4b, it is within
reach of the people in 3a, and its cost is nameable) is yours. Draft rounds: 1, Auditor
reads my brainstorm against my frame and names what it takes for granted and what it
misses; 2, Framer adds items I did not list, two lines each, with what each depends on;
3, Challenger takes the combined list and asks me, one at a time, whether each is viable
here and where it falls short, before saying which are generic; 4, Sensei asks which two
or three I will look at, what I need to learn about each, and gives the recap. Under an
[INTERIM] marker, the any-chatbot prompt, capped: three additions of two lines each, one
line per item on viability and where it falls short, one line naming the two or three
worth a real look.

**6. Three kinds of claim.** A reference page on the model of The problem frame:
definition, the three kinds, the rule that decides the check, two worked examples.

- A **checkable** claim is about what is now, and someone could confirm it by looking or
  by telling you. Check it by asking that person, or by looking.
- A **prediction** is about what will happen. Nobody can confirm it now. Check it by
  gathering signal: has it happened before, what happens in situations like it.
- A **causal bet** says one thing causes another. It hides a checkable claim inside a bet.
  Split it: check the present-tense part now, and hold the link as the bet a later change
  would test.

Worked example A sorts the handover assumptions list. Worked example B sorts the garden
list, and its causal bet does the teaching: "they quit because they had no one to ask"
splits into two checkable claims (they had no one to ask; they quit) and the link, which
stays a bet. The page answers "why care which kind" in one sentence: because the kind
tells you whether to ask, to look, or to wait.

**7. Concept check.** Five or six questions on the kinds page and the Introduction: which
kind is this claim; which check fits; a causal bet split correctly or not; the 3Cs applied
to the email example; what a verdict of no means. Written after the pages are drafted and
verified against them.

**8. What exists, and what it means.** The week 1 graded item, option A in the decisions.
Four parts, one submission: your brainstorm as you wrote it; what AI added and what you
rejected, in the form the spine requires (what you rejected, why, and what you lost by
rejecting it); the two or three you looked at, with what you learned about each and from
where; and what it means for your problem, in one paragraph. Three honest endings: nothing
out there fits, so the problem stands; something fits and adopting it here is the problem;
something fits and would simply solve it, so the problem is not worth nine weeks and the
runner-up comes into view. Handover example of real pushback: it offered "make handovers
a communication problem and train for it," which is probably partly right; dropped because
it points at training, and the frame says nobody owns the handover; may be wrong about
that. Closing step, optional: show your list and your pushback to someone outside the
course and record what they said. The verify line from the homepage metadata, surfaced.

**9. What a change would cost.** Before AI. Not the cost of any particular solution, which
would pull toward designing in week 4, but the cost of any change at all to how this is
handled: your own time, who in 3a or 4a would have to do something differently and what,
what breaks or gets worse while the change settles, and who would have to agree. Then put
it beside 3b, what the problem costs now, and say which is larger and how sure you are.
Short; one box. Handover example: every manager posts a one-line note when an account
moves, which costs each of them a minute and costs someone the nagging until it is a
habit; against clients repeating their history and days of stale ownership. Garden
example: pairing every new plot-holder with a returning one costs Ramona a matching job in
April and costs the returning gardeners their time; against six people quitting by
August.

**10. Dojo Lab: plan the checks.** Bring the assumptions list, every unverified entry, the
kinds page, the cost estimate, and the result of the one check if it has come back. Draft
rounds: 1, Auditor reads the list and names any assumption whose kind is wrong or whose
check does not fit its kind; 2, Challenger takes the causal bets one at a time and makes
the participant split each into the present-tense claim and the bet; 3, Challenger takes
the cost estimate and asks what it leaves out, one question at a time, then asks whether
the one check's result changes anything above; 4, Sensei asks which checks come first and
why, and gives the recap. What is mine vs what you carry: the assumptions, the cost
estimate, and the check's result are mine; the three kinds and their checks are yours.
The lab stops short of writing validation questions; that is Sprint 3's. Under an
[INTERIM] marker, the any-chatbot prompt, capped: three findings, ranked, naming the
assumption and the kind; one thing the cost estimate leaves out; one question someone who
works in the situation would ask.

**11. Confirmation Plan.** The terminal artifact, four parts, one submission, on the model
of the Problem Frame item.

Part 1, the Problem Frame brought up to date. Only where Sprint 2 changed it: 4a and 4b
if the research found something, part 6 with any assumption confirmed, added, or dropped.
A frame that did not change is a legitimate result, said in one line.

Part 2, the one check. The claim, what would have made it wrong, what you checked it
against and when, what came back: confirmed, contradicted, or complicated. Or what you
tried and why it could not be run.

Part 3, the plan. One row per unverified assumption: the assumption, its kind, the check
that fits, who could tell you or what you could observe. The break-the-frame assumptions
first. Causal bets appear as their split halves. No questions yet; Sprint 3 writes them.

Part 4, the verdict. Worth pursuing, not worth pursuing, or depends on one named thing,
with the reasons in one paragraph: what the check said, what exists, what a change would
cost. If the answer is no: which runner-up you are taking, its draft frame from First
frames, and a shortened pass, its two break-the-frame assumptions and one line on what
exists for it. The Introduction has told them this path is a result, not a failure.

Worked example: the handover plan, the check on the other managers come back
"complicated" (two of three describe it; the third says it only happens with one
outgoing manager), six rows sorted by kind, and a verdict of worth pursuing with the
reason.

**12. Reflection: what the world said back.** `ai_activity`, JSON upload, the Sprint 1
mechanism. Prompt: this sprint you checked one thing, looked at what exists, and priced
a change. Where did something outside your head change your view, and where did AI change
it? Which did you push back on, and what did it cost you to push back? And what is the one
thing in your plan you are least sure how to check? aiContext on the Sprint 1 pattern:
catch a pushback with nothing lost by it, a change attributed to AI that the participant
made, a "complicated" result rounded to confirmed, and a check that is really a to-do.
