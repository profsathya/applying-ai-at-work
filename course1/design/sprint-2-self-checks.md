# Sprint 2 Self-checks, v0.2

The rules are in `self-check-guide.md`. Rung points follow its rule: a 3 earns about 70 percent of the line, a 1 about 20 percent. Passing is 35 of 50. Lines are written for the learner; rungs are written as the sentence the learner receives in the grading comment.

## Dig into your assumptions, 50 points

Submission: one Word file, one table per assumption from Part 6 of the Problem Frame (three required, up to seven). Each table has four fields: My assumption, Questions behind my assumption, Different answers that could change my problem, How risky is this to shutting down my problem. Lines 2 to 5 are judged across every table submitted: full when every table passes, 3 when most do, 1 when few or none. Only line 1 counts tables.

Gate: fewer than two tables does not pass, whatever the other lines score.

| # | On the page | Pts | 3 rung (pts) | 1 rung (pts) | Rank | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | You submitted a table for every assumption in Part 6 of your Problem Frame, with each assumption pasted as it is written there. | 10 | Most assumptions have a table, and the ones that do are pasted as written. Every assumption gets a table, including the ones you are fairly sure of. (7) | Fewer than three tables, or assumptions reworded into something safer than what you actually assumed. (2) | 4 | completeness |
| 2 | In each table, your questions ask what the assumption takes for granted, not the assumption with a question mark on the end. | 10 | The questions on most tables ask something real; one or two restate the assumption. Ask what you would have to find out to know whether it holds. (7) | The questions restate the assumption. What would you have to learn to know if it is true? (2) | 3 | specificity |
| 3 | In each table, your possible answers include at least one that would change or end your problem. | 12 | Most tables have an answer that would change the problem; on some, every answer leads to "so I proceed." The answer you least want to hear is the one that belongs here. (9) | The answers all lead to the same place, or one formula is repeated on every table. Write down the answer that would make you drop this. (2) | 1 | judgment |
| 4 | In each table, your risk rating says how likely that answer is and why. | 12 | Most ratings give a reason; some give the rating alone or restate it. A rating without a reason is a guess with a label on it. (9) | Ratings with no reasons. Say why that answer is likely or unlikely. (2) | 2 | judgment |
| 5 | Your risk ratings differ from table to table, or you say why they are all the same. | 6 | Ratings are all one level, with reasons that mostly support them. Check whether every assumption really carries the same risk. (4) | Every table has the same rating and no reason, or the ratings contradict their reasons. (1) | 5 | honesty |

Total 50. Passing 35. Partial on every line gives 36.

**Grading note.** Every rung below full names the table and the field. Read for meaning. A blank field is a 1 on its line, not a 0 on the table.

**What makes it strong.** The answer that would end the problem is the one the learner least wants to hear, and it is written down anyway. The high-risk rows are the ones the Problem Frame's Status column carries forward, so a strong walk makes the taking-stock verdict almost write itself.

**Open, for the first-batch revision.** Whether "the assumption with a question mark on the end" is the common miss on line 2. Whether line 5 earns its place or folds into line 4.

## What solutions already exist, 50 points

Submission: one Word file, the three-column table (What exists, How it works as far as I know, What using it here would involve), at least three rows. Lines 2, 3, and 5 are judged across every row submitted. Line 1 counts rows; line 4 reads the set.

Gate: fewer than two rows does not pass, whatever the other lines score.

| # | On the page | Pts | 3 rung (pts) | 1 rung (pts) | Rank | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | You submitted at least three rows, and each one is something that already exists, such as a tool, a practice, a workaround, or a person, not something you would build. | 10 | Three rows, one of them something you would build. Keep what you would build for later; this table is about what is already there. (7) | Fewer than three rows, or mostly things you would build. (2) | 4 | completeness |
| 2 | In each row, "How it works" comes from what you have actually seen or read, and says "cannot tell yet" where you have not looked. | 12 | Most rows are from what you have seen; one or two are written from how it probably works. Mark those "cannot tell yet" until you have looked. (9) | The rows are guesses written as facts. Which of these have you actually seen working, and where? (2) | 1 | honesty |
| 3 | In each row, "What using it here would involve" says what would have to change for the people in your frame, not whether you like the option. | 12 | Most rows name a change for someone; some give an opinion of the option instead. Say who would have to do what differently. (9) | Opinions, or blanks, where the changes should be. Start from the people in Part 3a and say what each would have to take on. (2) | 2 | specificity |
| 4 | Your rows include at least one thing people already do to cope with this problem, and at least one thing from outside your situation. | 8 | Only one kind: all outside tools, or all local workarounds. Look in the other direction too. (6) | One kind only, and fewer than three rows. (2) | 5 | judgment |
| 5 | No row is marked as the answer, and your open questions and blanks are left standing. | 8 | One row is favoured before you have looked, but the questions are kept. Hold the verdict for the Problem Frame page. (6) | A row is chosen as the solution, or blanks are filled with guesses. (2) | 3 | judgment |

Total 50. Passing 35. Partial on every line gives 37.

**Grading note.** Every rung below full names the row and the column. Read for meaning. A blank cell is a 1 on its line, not a 0 on the row.

**What makes it strong.** Column 3 is where the Problem Frame's Part 3c comes from, so a strong column 3 names people and the habit or step they would have to take on. A row that says "cannot tell yet" in column 2 and names who could tell you is better than a confident row written from a product page.

**Open, for the first-batch revision.** Whether line 4 reads as a second completeness check; if so, drop it and split its points between lines 2 and 3.

## Problem Frame: taking stock of where you are, 50 points

Submission: three responses in one Canvas text entry under the page's three headings. Page edit to follow, as on the Sprint 1 Problem Frame page: the headings become Step 1, Step 2, Step 3, so they do not collide with the frame's Parts 1 to 7. Grading line 2 is easier with the learner's Sprint 1 Problem Frame open beside this one (Jeremy's grader, list item 46).

Gate: no pasted frame in Step 2 is not a pass, whatever Steps 1 and 3 score.

| # | On the page | Pts | 3 rung (pts) | 1 rung (pts) | Rank | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | In Step 1, you name at least one person and what fixing this problem would ask of them: a new step, a habit, time, or a decision. | 10 | A person is named and the ask is vague, or the ask is clear but not tied to a person. Say who, and what they would have to do differently. (7) | No person, or a solution described instead of what it asks of people. Go back to column 3 of your What exists table. (2) | 3 | specificity |
| 2 | In Step 2, your Problem Frame is pasted whole and brought up to date: it is dated, Part 3c is filled in, and every row of Part 6 has its Status column filled with a reason for the rating. | 12 | The frame is pasted and dated, with a few Status rows or reasons missing, or 3c thin. Every assumption needs a status and a reason; that is what your verdict stands on. (9) | Several parts missing, 3c blank, or Part 6 unchanged from Sprint 1. (2) | 2 | completeness |
| 3 | In Step 3, you give one of the three verdicts, and your reasons point to your risk ratings, your What exists table, and what the change would ask of people. | 12 | A verdict with reasons from one or two of the three. Your ratings, your table, and the ask of people each have something to say; use all three. (9) | A verdict with no reasons, or reasons from feeling alone. Which row in Part 6 or in your table is this verdict standing on? (2) | 1 | judgment |
| 4 | In Step 3, you say what is still uncertain and what would settle it. If your verdict is "depends on," you say what it depends on and who could settle it. If it is "no," you name your runner-up. | 10 | What is uncertain is named, without what would settle it, or a "depends on" without the who. Say what you would have to find out, and from whom. (7) | Nothing uncertain named, or a "depends on" with no condition, or a "no" with no runner-up. (2) | 4 | honesty |
| 5 | In Part 6, at least one assumption is rated high risk and still unconfirmed, or you say why none is. | 6 | A high-risk row is marked confirmed without saying how. Say what confirmed it. (4) | No high-risk row and no explanation. A frame with none at this stage is a warning sign; check your ratings. (1) | 5 | honesty |

Total 50. Passing 35. Partial on every line gives 36.

**Grading note.** A "no" with reasons this specific earns full marks on line 3, and the page says so. Every rung below full names the step and the part or row. Read for meaning. A blank step is a 1 on its lines.

**What makes it strong.** The verdict's reasons point at specific rows: a risk rating in Part 6, a row in What exists, a person in Step 1. "What would settle it" is the thing Sprint 3's stakeholder conversation goes and asks.

**Open, for the first-batch revision.** Whether line 5 earns its place at 6 points or folds into line 2. How often "no" appears, and whether the resubmission on the runner-up (list item 34) needs its own note here.
